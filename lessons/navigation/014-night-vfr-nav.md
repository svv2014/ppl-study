# Lesson NAV-014: Night VFR Navigation

**Section:** Navigation  
**Lesson number:** 014  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 9, CARs 602.115, CARs 605.16, CARs 605.17, AIM RAC 2.0

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson NAV-014. Flying at night is a different experience from flying in daylight — not just because you see less, but because the entire sensory environment changes. Ground features are obscured, depth perception is reduced, physiological effects on vision are significant, and the consequences of getting lost or disoriented are more serious. This lesson covers the legal definition of night in Canada, required equipment, weather minimums, airport beacon recognition, and the physiology of night vision.

---

**The Legal Definition of Night in Canada**

In Canada, "night" is defined in the Canadian Aviation Regulations as the period beginning **30 minutes after local sunset** and ending **30 minutes before local sunrise**.

This is important for several reasons:
- Night VFR currency requirements (a certain number of night takeoffs and landings to carry passengers at night)
- Additional aircraft equipment requirements
- Different fuel reserve requirements (45 minutes instead of 30 minutes)
- Different weather minimums

Note that civil twilight (when the sun is between 0° and 6° below the horizon) partially overlaps this definition — there is a brief period before and after official night during which reasonable visibility still exists. But from a regulatory standpoint, 30 minutes after sunset is when night procedures and requirements begin.

---

**Required Equipment for Night VFR**

Flying at night in Canada requires additional equipment beyond day VFR minimums.

**Position Lights (CARs 605.16):**

Position lights allow other aircraft to see you and determine your orientation. Canadian regulations require:
- **Red light on the left (port) wingtip**
- **Green light on the right (starboard) wingtip**
- **White light on the tail (rear)**

The colour assignment is the same as nautical convention: red-left, green-right. From a distance, if you see red on your left and green on your right, another aircraft is coming toward you. If you see only white, it's flying away from you.

**Anti-Collision Light (CARs 605.17):**

A flashing anti-collision light — either a red rotating beacon or a white strobe system — is required for night operations. The anti-collision light draws attention to the aircraft and is visible over a much greater range than position lights.

In practice, most modern training aircraft use strobe lights (white, high-intensity flashes) rather than rotating beacons. The strobes are typically activated whenever the aircraft is moving (or in some operations, whenever the engine is running).

**Landing Light:**

A landing light (forward-facing white light) is required under CARs for night operations conducted for hire. For private night VFR, a landing light is not technically required by CARs 605, but it is standard practice and strongly recommended for all night operations. It dramatically improves visibility during the landing flare and runway rollout, and makes the aircraft more visible to others during approach.

---

**Night VFR Weather Minimums**

The weather minimums for night VFR are more restrictive than day VFR because visual reference to the horizon and ground is reduced.

Under **CARs 602.115**, for flight in uncontrolled airspace at night:

- **Flight visibility: 3 statute miles (SM)**
- Cloud clearance requirements are the same as day VFR (500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, 2,000 feet horizontal from cloud in Class G airspace below 700 feet AGL)

For comparison, day VFR in uncontrolled airspace below 1,000 feet AGL allows 2 SM visibility. The night requirement is more conservative because maintaining visual ground reference in reduced visibility at night is significantly more difficult than in daylight.

In controlled airspace (Class C, D, or E), the general VFR minimum of 3 SM applies day and night.

---

**Night Chart Preparation**

Night navigation requires additional chart preparation focused on features visible at night:

**Airport Rotating Beacons:**

Canadian airports are equipped with rotating beacons that operate from sunset to sunrise (and during IFR conditions in the daytime). The beacon colours indicate the airport type:

| Beacon Colour | Airport Type |
|--------------|-------------|
| White + Green (alternating) | Land airport (civil) |
| White + White (two whites per rotation) | Military airport |
| White + Yellow (alternating) | Water aerodrome (seaplane base) |

Being able to recognize these colours at night is essential for confirming you've found the correct airport. Fly toward a rotating beacon to home in on an airport when using visual navigation at night.

**Lighted Obstructions:**

Communication towers and tall structures have red obstruction lights (flashing or steady, depending on height). High-intensity white strobes are used on the tallest structures. At night, these lights become significant navigation hazards — know their locations on the chart.

**Town and Highway Lighting:**

Towns and cities are visible at night as clusters of lights. Major highways appear as strings of headlights and streetlights. These are useful night landmarks but require more care in identification — a small town's lights can look similar to a larger town's from altitude.

---

**Physiology of Night Vision**

Flying at night requires understanding how human vision works in low light, because it is significantly different from daytime vision.

**Rods and Cones:**

The human eye has two types of photoreceptors:

- **Cones** are concentrated at the centre of the retina (the fovea) and provide high-resolution colour vision. They require relatively bright light to function. During the day, cones do most of the work.
- **Rods** are distributed around the periphery of the retina and are extremely sensitive to light — about 10,000 times more sensitive than cones. However, they provide only black-and-white (achromatic) vision, and they have very poor resolution. Rods are the night vision system.

**Off-Centre Viewing:**

Because rods are concentrated away from the fovea, a pilot looking directly at a dim object at night will place the image on the cone-rich fovea — and the object may become invisible. To see dim objects at night (a faint runway edge light, a distant aircraft light, a barely-lit obstacle), look approximately **10° to the side** of the object. This places the image on the rod-dense region of the retina and makes the object visible. This technique is called **off-centre viewing** or **averted vision**.

**Dark Adaptation:**

It takes approximately **30 minutes** for the eyes to fully adapt to darkness after bright light exposure. The rhodopsin (visual purple) pigment in the rods must regenerate. Any bright light exposure — instrument lights, cockpit lighting, phone screens — resets the adaptation process in that eye.

To preserve dark adaptation:
- Use red cockpit lighting (red light does not bleach rhodopsin significantly)
- Avoid looking at bright instrument displays
- If one eye is exposed to bright light, the other eye retains its adaptation

**Night Myopia:**

In very low light conditions, the eye tends to focus at a resting focal distance of approximately 1–3 metres — not infinity. This means in featureless dark skies, you may not be focused at the distance needed to detect distant lights. Periodically scanning and focusing on the instrument panel (which is in focus) and then the horizon helps maintain appropriate visual scanning.

---

**Hypoxia and Night Vision**

Night vision is significantly impaired by hypoxia — oxygen deficiency. Rod cells are among the most oxygen-sensitive cells in the body. At altitudes above 5,000–6,000 feet ASL, night vision begins to deteriorate noticeably, even in a pilot who feels fine.

For night flights above 5,000 feet ASL, supplemental oxygen is advisable even though it is not legally required at those altitudes for single-pilot operations. At 10,000 feet ASL, night vision impairment from hypoxia can be equivalent to looking through a filter that reduces light by 40%.

---

**Night Navigation Technique**

At night, the balance of navigation methods shifts:
- **Pilotage** is limited to town lights, rotating beacons, and major highway lighting — far fewer landmarks than daylight
- **Dead reckoning** becomes more important — fly precise headings and accurate timings
- **Radio navigation** (VOR, NDB) is particularly valuable at night because it doesn't depend on visual ground reference
- **GPS** is extremely helpful for situational awareness, especially the moving map

Plan night cross-country routes to pass over or near identifiable lighted areas and airports. Avoid routes that pass over large dark areas (unlit boreal forest, large lakes) without any radio navigation aid coverage.
