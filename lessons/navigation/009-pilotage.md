# Lesson NAV-009: Pilotage — Navigating by Ground Reference

**Section:** Navigation  
**Lesson number:** 009  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 9

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson NAV-009. Before there was GPS, before there were VORs, pilots navigated primarily by looking out the window. They matched what they saw below them to what was printed on the chart. That technique is called pilotage — and it remains the most direct and intuitive form of navigation for VFR pilots today.

Pilotage does not replace dead reckoning or radio aids; it works alongside them. A good VFR pilot uses all three together. This lesson explains how to select checkpoints, read the ground, and maintain your track when features are scarce.

---

**What is Pilotage?**

Pilotage is the art of navigating by visually identifying natural and man-made features on the ground and comparing them to their representation on the aeronautical chart. It is purely visual — no instruments beyond a clock and chart are strictly required.

The process is fundamentally a matching game:
1. Know approximately where you are (from dead reckoning)
2. Look at the chart for distinctive features near your projected position
3. Look out the window for those features
4. When you see a feature that matches the chart, you have confirmed your position

---

**Selecting Good Checkpoints**

Not all landmarks are equally useful. Good checkpoints are:

**Distinctive** — they stand out from the surrounding terrain and can't be confused with similar features nearby. A large lake is more distinctive than one of dozens of small lakes. A city with a distinctive shape is more useful than a small village.

**Identifiable from the air** — some features that look prominent on a chart are invisible from altitude. A small river through flat farmland may disappear into the landscape. A major railway junction is clearly visible from 3,000 feet.

**On or near your planned track** — the best checkpoints are things you fly directly over or within a NM or two. Checkpoints 10 NM off track require you to identify them by shape and relative position, which is harder.

**Spaced appropriately** — checkpoints every 15–25 NM give you regular position confirmations without overwhelming you with information.

---

**Types of Checkpoints**

Here are features commonly used for VFR pilotage in Canada:

- **Towns and cities** — recognizable from their road networks, buildings, and often airport symbols. Larger towns have distinctive shapes visible from altitude.
- **Rivers and lakes** — excellent because their shapes are unique and match the chart precisely. The intersection of two rivers is especially distinctive.
- **Railways** — appear as straight lines with periodic small structures (stations, sidings). A railway crossing your track perpendicular to it is an excellent "line feature" for confirming along-track position.
- **Major highways** — multi-lane divided highways are visible from cruise altitude. Interchanges are particularly distinctive.
- **Coastlines** — extremely reliable in coastal areas. Match the coastline shape to the chart.
- **Terrain features** — ridgelines, prominent hills, valleys. Useful in hilly terrain; less useful in flat areas.
- **Airports** — easy to spot on a clear day, especially paved runways. Your own destination airport is the ultimate checkpoint.

---

**The Chart-to-Ground Matching Process**

When you're approaching a checkpoint:

1. Look at the chart 2–3 minutes before you expect to arrive at it
2. Note the shape, orientation, and surrounding features of the checkpoint
3. Scan the horizon in the expected direction
4. Identify the feature — confirm by checking secondary details (e.g., a lake with an island, a town beside a river)
5. Update your flight log: actual time over checkpoint, revised ETA for next checkpoint

Do not stare at the chart trying to identify ambiguous features. If a checkpoint is unclear after 30 seconds of searching, continue on dead reckoning heading and look for the next checkpoint. Fixating on one ambiguous feature while flying is more dangerous than a brief period of uncertainty.

---

**Maintaining Track Between Checkpoints**

Between checkpoints, you maintain your track by flying the correct compass heading (from your PLOG) and timing the leg. However, winds can push you off track. Periodic checks help catch drift early.

**Track monitoring from the air:**

Look for features that you should be flying directly over or parallel to. If your planned track runs alongside a railway, the railway should stay parallel to your wing — if it's drifting to one side, you've drifted off track.

Look for your next checkpoint in the direction it should appear. If it appears to the left of where expected, you've drifted to the right.

---

**The 1-in-60 Rule**

The 1-in-60 rule is a quick way to estimate track error and correction angles:

**At 60 NM from the last checkpoint, 1 NM of off-track displacement = 1° of track error**

More generally: **Track Error Angle = (Off-track distance ÷ Distance flown) × 60**

Example: You've flown 40 NM since your last checkpoint and you're 3 NM off track to the right.

Track Error Angle = (3 ÷ 40) × 60 = 4.5° ≈ 5°

To correct and return to track by the next checkpoint 30 NM ahead:

Correction angle = Track Error (5°) + Closing Angle = 5° + (3 ÷ 30) × 60 ≈ 5° + 6° = 11°

Turn 11° to the left to intercept your original track by the next checkpoint.

For the PPL exam, a simplified version is often used: if you're off track, double the track error angle to get back on track over a remaining equal distance. This gives a rough but usable correction.

---

**Limitations of Pilotage**

Pilotage breaks down in several situations:

**Low visibility** — haze, smoke, rain, or fog reduces the visibility of ground features. Even at legal VFR minimums (3 SM), pilotage can be difficult if the horizon is hazy and features are washed out. In those conditions, dead reckoning and radio aids become more important.

**Featureless terrain** — large areas of flat prairie, tundra, boreal forest, or open water offer few distinctive landmarks. Individual fields look the same; boreal lakes may be numerous and similar in shape. Checkpoints may be 50–80 NM apart. Dead reckoning accuracy becomes critical.

**Night** — visible ground features are limited to lighted towns, airports with rotating beacons, and occasionally highways. Night pilotage requires more reliance on dead reckoning and radio aids.

**High speed** — at high ground speeds, landmarks appear and pass quickly. At 200+ knots, the time between checkpoints may be only a few minutes. This is primarily a jet concern, not relevant to most PPL training aircraft.

---

**Combining Pilotage with Dead Reckoning**

The professional approach is to use both together:
- Dead reckoning provides a predicted position at every moment
- Pilotage confirms or corrects that position at each checkpoint

When they agree, you have high confidence in your position.  
When they disagree, investigate: Did you fly the correct heading? Is the ground speed what you expected? Have you correctly identified the landmark?

Never abandon your dead reckoning plan and start "chasing" random landmarks — this is a common cause of becoming lost. Maintain your heading and use the DR estimate as your primary position until you can make a positive identification.

## Quick Reference: Pilotage Summary

| Good Checkpoint Characteristics | Poor Checkpoint Characteristics |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Distinctive, unique shape | Common, easily confused |
| On or near planned track | Far off-track |
| Visible from cruise altitude | Small or featureless |
| Perpendicular line features | Parallel to track (ambiguous distance) |
