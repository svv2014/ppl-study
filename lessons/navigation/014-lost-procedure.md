# Lesson NAV-014: Lost Procedure and Diversion

**Section:** Navigation  
**Lesson number:** 014  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 9, AIM RAC 3.0, CARs 602.31

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson NAV-014. No pilot plans to get lost. But in aviation, as in all skilled endeavours, things don't always go as planned. Visibility drops, landmarks don't match the chart, the E6B was read wrong, and suddenly you are not sure exactly where you are. This lesson teaches you what to do in those moments — and what to do when weather ahead makes your planned destination unreachable and you need to divert.

---

**The Difference Between "Uncertain" and "Lost"**

There is an important distinction between being **uncertain of position** and being **lost**. A pilot who is uncertain of position suspects they may have drifted off track but has a reasonable idea of their general area. A pilot who is genuinely lost has no reliable idea of their position.

The procedures are similar but the urgency differs. Uncertainty is common and manageable. Being genuinely lost for extended periods, especially with decreasing fuel, is a serious situation requiring ATC assistance.

---

**Prevention is the Best Procedure**

Before discussing what to do when lost, note that good habits prevent it:

1. **Fly a planned route** — pre-flight PLOG with checkpoints every 15–30 NM
2. **Time your checkpoints** — if you're 5 minutes past your ETA and haven't spotted the checkpoint, investigate
3. **Maintain heading** — most "lost" situations result from undetected drift; fly the compass heading you calculated
4. **Keep the chart oriented** — rotate the chart so north on the chart aligns with north on the ground

If you are diligently timing checkpoints and verifying ground features, you will rarely become lost.

---

**The 5 Cs — What to Do When Lost**

Transport Canada teaches the **5 Cs** for lost pilots:

**1. Climb**

Gain altitude (within airspace and fuel limits). Higher altitude gives you:
- A wider view of the ground, making landmark identification easier
- Better radio reception (line-of-sight radio range increases with altitude)
- More time to assess the situation

**2. Circle**

Stop travelling in an unknown direction. Orbit your current position. This prevents you from flying deeper into unfamiliar or hazardous territory while you work on identifying your position.

**3. Confess**

Admit you are lost — to yourself and to ATC. The instinct to keep flying forward and hope recognition comes is dangerous. Pride has contributed to many accidents. Confessing the situation early opens options; waiting too long until fuel is critical closes them.

**4. Communicate**

Contact ATC, an FSS (Flight Service Station), or use **121.5 MHz** — the international emergency and guard frequency, monitored continuously by ATC and military facilities. State your situation, and if known, your last confirmed position and fuel remaining.

ATC can use radar to identify your transponder return and provide your position, then vector you to a suitable airport.

**5. Comply**

Follow ATC instructions. When being vectored to an airport, maintain the headings and altitudes given. Do not deviate unless safety requires it.

---

**Transponder Codes**

When lost and becoming an emergency:

| Code | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| 7000 | Canadian VFR standby (no assigned code) |
| 7700 | Emergency (includes being lost and in distress) |
| 7600 | Radio failure |
| 7500 | Hijack (never use accidentally) |

Squawk 7700 alerts ATC immediately and increases the urgency of response. Do not hesitate to use it if you are genuinely unable to navigate safely.

---

**Attempting to Re-establish Position**

Before calling ATC, try to identify your position using the following systematic approach:

**Step 1 — Identify Large Features**

Look for major landmarks that would appear on the VNC:
- Large lakes, rivers, or coastlines
- Cities or large towns
- Railways, highways, or power lines
- Prominent hills or terrain features

These features are large enough to identify even with significant navigation error.

**Step 2 — Establish Time Since Last Known Position**

When did you last verify your position with certainty? How far could you have flown since then (GS × time)? Draw a circle of uncertainty on your chart centred on your last confirmed position with a radius equal to maximum possible distance since then. You are somewhere inside that circle.

**Step 3 — Look for Matching Combinations**

Look for combinations of features — a town near a bend in a river, or a lake at a particular orientation relative to a highway. A single feature may not be unique; a combination of two or three features usually is.

**Step 4 — Follow a Line Feature**

If you cannot identify position, pick a long linear feature (major highway, river, railway) and follow it to a named point (city, aerodrome, junction) where you can confirm position.

---

**Diversion to an Alternate**

A diversion is a planned change of destination — to an alternate aerodrome — when the original destination becomes unsuitable due to weather, mechanical issues, or other factors.

**Steps for an In-Flight Diversion:**

**1. Identify a suitable alternate**

Open your VNC and find an aerodrome within comfortable range given remaining fuel. Prefer:
- Paved runway (unless you have field landing capability)
- Runway long enough for your aircraft
- Weather conditions at the alternate better than at your original destination
- Fuel available (check CAP for fuel availability)

**2. Estimate the track**

Place your thumb at your present position and rotate the chart to estimate the approximate magnetic track to the alternate. A rough estimate to within 10° is sufficient to get started; refine it in flight.

**3. Calculate fuel**

Quick mental estimate: remaining fuel ÷ burn rate = endurance remaining (hours). Time to alternate = distance ÷ GS. Ensure endurance exceeds time to alternate plus 30-minute reserve.

**4. Notify ATC or FSS**

Inform ATC of your diversion intention if you have an active flight plan or are in communication with ATC. If you filed a flight plan for the original destination, close it and open a new one for the alternate (or update ATC). This prevents unnecessary SAR activation.

**5. Fly to the alternate**

Maintain altitude and fly the estimated track. Update your heading as you refine your track and GS estimates.

---

**Weather Deterioration Scenarios**

The most common reason for diversion is weather ahead. When you encounter deteriorating weather:

- **Do not continue into IMC (Instrument Meteorological Conditions)** if you are VFR-only. This is the leading cause of VFR into IMC accidents.
- Make the decision to divert **early** — the time between "this looks marginal" and "I cannot turn back" can be very short.
- A 180° turn while you still have good VMC behind you is almost always available. Once in IMC, it is not.

---

**Exam Tips**

The PPL written exam tests:
- The 5 Cs in order (Climb, Circle, Confess, Communicate, Comply)
- Transponder codes (7700 = emergency, 7600 = radio failure, 7500 = hijack)
- Frequency 121.5 MHz as the guard/emergency frequency
- Decision-making in diversion scenarios

## Quick Reference: Lost Procedure and Diversion

**5 Cs:** Climb → Circle → Confess → Communicate → Comply

**Transponder codes:**
- 7700: Emergency
- 7600: Radio failure
- 7500: Hijack

**Emergency frequency:** 121.5 MHz (guard frequency, monitored at all times)

**Diversion steps:**
1. Identify suitable alternate on chart
2. Estimate track and check fuel
3. Notify ATC / FSS
4. Fly to alternate

**Key rule:** Never continue VFR into IMC. Turn back or divert before conditions trap you.
