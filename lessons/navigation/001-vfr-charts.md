# Lesson NAV-001: VFR Aeronautical Charts — Reading the Map

**Section:** Navigation  
**Lesson number:** 001  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 9, Transport Canada VFR Navigation Charts (VNC) legend, TP 9994 (VFR Chart User's Guide)

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Navigation. Before you can plot a course, calculate a wind correction angle, or plan a cross-country trip, you need to be able to read the chart. This lesson covers the VFR Aeronautical Charts that Canadian pilots use — what they show, how they're organized, and what the symbols mean.

---

**What Chart Do Canadian VFR Pilots Use?**

In Canada, the primary chart for VFR cross-country navigation is the **VNC — VFR Navigation Chart**. VNCs are published by NAV CANADA and cover the country at a scale of 1:500,000. That means one centimetre on the chart equals five kilometres on the ground. This scale is large enough to show towns, lakes, and rivers clearly, but small enough to cover a usable chunk of territory on a single sheet.

For flying around airports and in terminal areas, pilots use the **VTA — VFR Terminal Area Chart**, which is at a larger scale of 1:250,000 — more detail in a smaller area. For the written exam, you'll mostly be tested on VNC content.

---

**Scale and Distance**

The 1:500,000 scale means you can measure distances directly with a ruler. If two airports are 4 centimetres apart on the chart, they're 20 nautical miles apart on the ground — because one centimetre equals 5 kilometres, and 1 nautical mile is approximately 1.852 kilometres.

A better shortcut for the exam: most VNCs include a graphic scale at the bottom. You can use the scale bar directly with your navigation plotter, which is the plastic ruler-and-protractor combination that pilots use for chart work.

---

**Latitude and Longitude**

VNCs use a grid of latitude and longitude lines. Latitude lines run east-west and measure how far north or south you are from the equator. Longitude lines run north-south and measure how far east or west from the Prime Meridian at Greenwich.

On Canadian VNCs, latitude and longitude lines are printed every 30 minutes of arc — that's half a degree. Each degree of latitude equals approximately 60 nautical miles. So if two points are one degree of latitude apart, they're roughly 60 nautical miles apart. This is a useful mental shortcut.

---

**Terrain and Elevation**

The chart uses colours and contour lines to show terrain. Brown and green tones indicate different elevation bands — darker brown means higher terrain. There are contour lines at set intervals, and spot elevations are shown in feet. The highest point in any 30-minute grid square is shown with a larger bold number — this is the **MEF, or Maximum Elevation Figure**, and it's critical for terrain clearance planning. The MEF already includes a buffer above the highest terrain or obstacle in that square. When planning your cruise altitude, you want to be above the MEF.

---

**Airports**

Airports on VNCs are shown with specific symbols depending on their type:

- **Blue circle with tick marks** — airport with a control tower (ATC services)
- **Yellow circle** — airport without a tower (uncontrolled)
- **Magenta circle** — seaplane base
- **Hard-surface runways** are shown as a solid cross or elongated rectangle; grass or unpaved runways are shown with open symbols

Next to each airport symbol you'll see a text block. It typically shows the airport's elevation in feet, the length of the longest runway, and a frequency — usually the CTAF (Common Traffic Advisory Frequency) or the tower frequency.

---

**Airspace**

Airspace boundaries are shown as lines and shaded areas:

- **Solid blue line** — Class C airspace boundary
- **Dashed blue line** — Class D airspace boundary
- **Dashed magenta line** — Class E surface area (around some smaller airports)
- **Blue-and-white hatch** — Class B airspace layer
- **Solid blue shading at low level** — this often indicates areas where Class E or special airspace begins at lower altitudes

The airspace ceilings and floors are shown in hundreds of feet, formatted as a fraction. For example, **40/SFC** means from the surface (SFC) up to 4,000 feet ASL. The top number is the ceiling, bottom is the floor.

---

**Obstructions**

Tall towers and obstacles are shown as small symbols — a solid dot with a line pointing up for structures under 1,000 feet AGL, and a different symbol for structures over 1,000 feet AGL. The elevation of the top of the obstacle is shown in feet MSL beside the symbol, with the height AGL in brackets.

---

**Navigational Aids (NAVAIDs)**

VHF Omnidirectional Range stations — VORs — are shown as a compass-rose circle with a dot in the centre. The VOR's frequency, identifier, and Morse code are printed next to it. NDBs (Non-Directional Beacons) are shown as a purple or magenta circle with a dot. You don't need to operate these on the written exam but you need to recognize them on the chart.

---

**Magnetic Variation**

One of the most important lines on the chart is the **isogonic line** — or line of magnetic variation. It's a dashed purple or magenta line that runs roughly north-south across the chart. Each line shows how many degrees of difference there are between True North (toward the geographic North Pole) and Magnetic North (where the compass points). In eastern Canada, the variation is typically 10–15 degrees West, meaning magnetic north is west of true north. You'll need this value when converting between true headings and magnetic headings — a Navigation topic we'll cover in detail in later lessons.

---

**Putting It Together**

Before a cross-country flight, you'll use the VNC to: find your departure and destination airports, trace a route avoiding controlled airspace, check MEFs along the route, note any VORs or NDBs you might use, and measure the distance and true heading of each leg. The chart is your primary pre-flight planning document.

For the written exam, you'll be given a chart excerpt and asked to identify symbols, read airspace floors and ceilings, or extract an airport's CTAF frequency. Spend time with a real VNC before your exam — the legend is printed on every chart sheet.

## Quick Reference: Key Chart Symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Blue circle + ticks | Controlled airport (tower) |
| Yellow circle | Uncontrolled airport |
| Solid blue line | Class C boundary |
| Dashed blue line | Class D boundary |
| Dashed magenta line | Class E surface area |
| Dashed purple/magenta line | Isogonic line (magnetic variation) |
| Compass rose + dot | VOR |
| Purple dot circle | NDB |
| Bold number in grid square | MEF (Maximum Elevation Figure) |
