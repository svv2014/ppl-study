# Lesson NAV-013: Lost Procedures and Navigation Emergencies

**Section:** Navigation  
**Lesson number:** 013  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 9, AIM RAC 3.14, CARs 602.138

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson NAV-013. Every pilot should hope never to be genuinely lost — but every pilot should know exactly what to do if it happens. Getting lost is not an emergency in itself. It becomes an emergency only if you allow it to escalate: running low on fuel, entering cloud, or descending into terrain. With the right procedure, a lost pilot can almost always resolve the situation safely.

---

**The PLAN Procedure**

When you realize you are uncertain of your position, use the PLAN procedure:

**P — Position: Estimate where you are**  
Before anything else, stop and think. What was your last confirmed position? How long have you been flying since then? At your planned ground speed, how far could you have traveled? Draw a mental (or actual) circle on your chart centered on that estimated position with a radius equal to your maximum range from that last fix. You must be somewhere inside that circle.

**L — Lost? (or Looks OK?)**  
Honestly assess how uncertain you are. "I'm not sure exactly which town that is" is not the same as "I have no idea where I am." If you have a rough idea, use it. Look out the window for distinctive features — a major highway, a river, a distinctive lakeshore — and try to match them to your chart.

**A — Aviate**  
Maintain aircraft control. Keep flying straight and level at a safe altitude. This sounds obvious but stress can cause fixation on the chart while the aircraft drifts or descends. Aviate first — navigate second.

**N — Navigate**  
Take systematic action. We'll walk through the specific steps below.

---

**Step 1: Maintain Heading and Check the Flight Log**

Do not make random turns. Random turns increase your uncertainty and consume fuel. Fly the last known heading and consult your navigation log. Your flight log (PLOG) shows your planned heading, ground speed, and time for each leg. Count forward from your last checkpoint: if 25 minutes have elapsed and your ground speed is 100 knots, you've traveled approximately 42 NM.

Project that position on your chart. Look out the window for something that matches.

---

**Step 2: Climb if Safe**

Climbing increases your visual range, extends VOR and NDB reception, and may help you spot distinctive landmarks you couldn't see at lower altitude. Before climbing:
- Check for cloud — do not climb into cloud if not instrument rated
- Check the MEF for your estimated area — ensure the climb is safe
- A climb of even 1,000–2,000 feet can significantly improve situational awareness

---

**Step 3: Look for Landmarks**

Concentrate on large, distinctive features:
- Major cities or towns (distinctive road patterns, industrial areas)
- Rivers and lakes (match the shape to the chart)
- Coastlines (extremely reliable)
- Railways (straight lines with stations)
- Major highways (multi-lane, distinctive interchanges)

Match what you see to the chart. Look for combinations of features that are unique — a lake with a river outlet, a town at the junction of two highways, an airport with a distinctive runway layout.

---

**Step 4: Use Radio Navigation Aids**

If airborne within VOR or NDB range, use them to fix your position:

**VOR:** Tune to a VOR whose position you know. Centre the CDI with the FROM flag. Read the OBS — that is your radial from that station. Draw that radial on your chart. Then tune a second VOR and get a second bearing. Plot both radials — you are at or near their intersection.

**NDB:** Tune an NDB, identify it by Morse code, and take a bearing (Magnetic Heading + Relative Bearing = Magnetic Bearing TO the NDB). Plot the reciprocal from the NDB position on the chart to get a position line.

---

**Step 5: Contact ATC — 121.5 MHz**

**121.5 MHz** is the international aeronautical emergency and distress frequency. It is monitored 24 hours a day by:
- All ATC units in Canada
- Military radar stations
- Many commercial aircraft overhead
- NAV CANADA flight service stations

If you are lost, transmit on 121.5 MHz:
- "MAYDAY" (or "PAN PAN" if not an emergency) × 3
- Aircraft type and registration
- Position (last known) and situation
- Altitude and fuel remaining
- Number of persons on board

ATC can provide a **DF steer** — Direction Finding assistance using your radio transmissions to determine your bearing from their station. This can pinpoint your position very quickly.

You can also ask ATC to **identify your transponder** (squawk ident) to locate you on radar.

---

**Step 6: Squawk 7700**

In a genuine emergency (lost AND low fuel, or lost AND weather deteriorating around you), squawk **7700** on the transponder. This triggers immediate alerts at ATC radar facilities and ensures maximum attention is directed to your situation.

Know all three emergency transponder codes:
- **7700** — General emergency
- **7600** — Radio communication failure (NORDO)
- **7500** — Unlawful interference / hijack

---

**SARTIME and VFR Flight Itinerary**

Before departure on any cross-country flight, Canadian pilots should file either:

**VFR Flight Plan** (with NAV CANADA): When the plan is overdue at the destination, NAV CANADA initiates SARTIME procedures after 30 minutes. If the flight plan is not closed, SAR alerting begins.

**Flight Itinerary**: If a flight plan is not filed (or cannot be filed), leave a detailed itinerary with a **responsible person** — someone who will call NAV CANADA at the SARTIME if they haven't heard from you. The responsible person needs: your route, altitude, aircraft description, departure time, and the SARTIME. Per **CARs 602.138**, you must close or check in by the SARTIME or SAR will be activated.

The SARTIME is the single most important piece of rescue infrastructure a VFR pilot has. If you crash, someone is looking for you — but only if you filed.

---

**ELT — Emergency Locator Transmitter**

Most Canadian aircraft are required to carry an ELT (Emergency Locator Transmitter) under **CARs 605.38** (or the specific aircraft equipment regulations). The ELT activates automatically on impact (G-force trigger) and transmits on 406 MHz (modern ELTs) or 121.5 MHz (older units). The 406 MHz signal is picked up by the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system and relays your position to rescue coordination centres.

Know how to manually activate your aircraft's ELT if you land in a remote area. Also know how to deactivate it after inadvertent activation — an ELT going off in the hangar triggers a SAR response.

---

**Summary: Lost Procedure in Order**

1. Maintain heading — do not wander
2. Check flight log — estimate position from last fix
3. Climb if safe — expand visual and radio horizon
4. Look for landmarks — match to chart
5. Tune VOR or NDB — plot position lines
6. Transmit on 121.5 MHz — request DF steer or radar identification
7. Squawk 7700 if genuine emergency
8. If terrain-safe, divert toward populated area, major highway, or coast

## Quick Reference: Emergency Transponder Codes and Frequencies

| Code | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| 7700 | General emergency |
| 7600 | Radio failure (NORDO) |
| 7500 | Hijack / unlawful interference |
| 1200 | Standard VFR (Canada) |

| Frequency | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| 121.5 MHz | International emergency frequency |
| 126.7 MHz | En route advisory (NAV CANADA) |
| 122.8 MHz | Typical CTAF (uncontrolled airports) |
