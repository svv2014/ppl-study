# Lesson AL-002: Controlled vs Uncontrolled Airspace

**Section:** Air Law  
**Lesson number:** 002  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** CARs Part VI (Airspace), TP 12880E Chapter 7, AIM RAC 2.0

---

## Narration Script

Welcome back to Air Law. Last lesson you learned the airspace classes — A through G. Now we go deeper into the most important dividing line in aviation: the difference between controlled and uncontrolled airspace, and what that means for you as a VFR pilot.

---

**The Big Divide**

Canadian airspace splits into two fundamental categories: controlled and uncontrolled.

Controlled airspace — Classes A, B, C, D, and E — is airspace where Air Traffic Control provides services. ATC is active, watching radar, sequencing aircraft, and in most cases talking to you. The word "controlled" doesn't mean you're being controlled like a puppet — it means ATC has authority and responsibility in that airspace.

Uncontrolled airspace — Class G — is airspace where ATC provides no services. No radar. No separation. You're responsible for seeing and avoiding other traffic yourself. The word "uncontrolled" can sound scary, but it's not. Most general aviation flying in Canada happens in Class G, particularly in rural and low-altitude areas.

---

**What ATC Actually Does in Controlled Airspace**

The services ATC provides depend on the class of airspace.

In Class A and B — the high-altitude IFR world — ATC provides full separation between all aircraft. Every aircraft is being tracked and managed. You won't be in Class A or B as a VFR student pilot.

In Class C — around the busiest airports — ATC provides separation between IFR aircraft, and between IFR and VFR aircraft. If you're flying VFR through Class C, ATC is keeping IFR traffic away from you. They also issue traffic advisories to help you see and avoid other VFR aircraft — but they don't separate VFR from VFR.

In Class D — around towered airports that don't qualify for Class C — ATC provides separation for IFR aircraft, and traffic information for VFR aircraft. There's no ATC separation between VFR and VFR in Class D. The controller will tell you about traffic, but you're responsible for avoiding it.

In Class E — controlled airspace at lower altitudes, often around smaller airports or along airways — ATC provides separation for IFR aircraft only. VFR pilots get no separation services. You're in controlled airspace, but ATC isn't watching out for you specifically.

---

**Pilot Responsibilities in Each Zone**

This is critical for the exam: your responsibility for traffic avoidance changes depending on where you are.

In Class C: ATC keeps IFR traffic away from you. But VFR-versus-VFR is your problem. Eyes outside the cockpit.

In Class D: ATC gives you information. Action is yours.

In Class E: ATC is focused on IFR. You're on your own for traffic avoidance.

In Class G: No ATC at all. You see and avoid everything.

The pattern is clear: the higher the class letter, the less ATC help you get as a VFR pilot. Remember this: in all airspace, the see-and-avoid responsibility never fully goes away.

---

**Radio Requirements**

Communication requirements change with airspace class.

Class A and B: radio required, ATC clearance required — but you won't be here as a VFR student.

Class C: radio required, two-way contact established before entry, ATC clearance required.

Class D: radio required, two-way contact before entry — but no formal clearance. The controller must acknowledge your call sign.

Class E: no radio requirement for VFR pilots. You can enter Class E without talking to anyone.

Class G: no radio requirement at all. That said, if you're flying near an uncontrolled airport, you should still be transmitting on the CTAF — Common Traffic Advisory Frequency — to announce your position to other traffic. It's not required by law in all situations, but it's required by common sense.

---

**Transponder Requirements**

A transponder is the radio device in your aircraft that responds to ATC radar interrogations and lets controllers see your altitude.

In Class A and B: transponder with Mode C (altitude encoding) required.

In Class C and D: transponder with Mode C required when operating within or through those areas.

In Class E and G: no transponder required — unless you're above a certain altitude or in specific areas as defined in the CARs.

For the exam, remember: transponder with Mode C is mandatory inside Class C and Class D. Outside controlled airspace, it's optional unless specific rules apply.

---

**The Special Case of Class E**

Class E trips up a lot of students because it sits in a grey zone. It's controlled airspace — but you don't need a clearance, you don't need radio, and ATC isn't separating you from anything. So why is it "controlled"?

The answer is: controlled means IFR aircraft in that airspace are receiving ATC services. The classification is about what ATC provides to IFR aircraft — not what it provides to you as a VFR pilot. When the exam asks about controlled airspace, remember that Class E is in that category, even though it feels a lot like Class G for a VFR pilot.

---

**Practical Takeaway**

Before every cross-country flight, check the VNC chart for the airspace along your route. Know which classes you'll be transiting. If you're going through Class C, you need a clearance and radio contact. If you're going through Class D, you need radio contact. If you're staying in Class G, none of that applies — but see and avoid is always on.

The more urban your route, the more controlled airspace you'll encounter. The more rural and low-altitude your flying, the more time you spend in Class G.
