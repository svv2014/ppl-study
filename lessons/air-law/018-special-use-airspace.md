# Lesson AL-018: Special Use Airspace — Restricted, Class F(R), MOAs, ADIZs

**Section:** Air Law

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson AL-018, the final lesson in the Air Law section. This lesson covers special use airspace — the areas of Canadian airspace set aside for specific activities, hazards, or security purposes that require pilots to take extra steps before or during flight.

---

### Class F Airspace — Two Subtypes

We introduced Class F briefly in Lesson AL-001. Now we go deeper.

Class F airspace in Canada is established for areas where activities or conditions make it necessary to restrict, limit, or advise concerning the use of airspace. There are two subtypes:

**Class F(R) — Restricted**

Class F Restricted airspace requires specific authorization to enter. The controlling authority (Transport Canada, DND, or another government agency) grants permission on a case-by-case basis, or for specific operations. Entry without authorization is a violation of CARs 601.15.

Examples of Class F(R):
- Active military weapons ranges
- Areas used for live firing
- Restricted security areas (certain government facilities)
- Active nuclear sites

A Class F(R) area has a designation starting with **CYR** (Canadian, Restricted) followed by a number — for example, CYR 537. When a CYR area is active, you must have authorization to enter.

**Class F(A) — Advisory**

Class F Advisory airspace does not require authorization. It exists to advise pilots of activities that may pose a hazard. Pilots are strongly encouraged to check NOTAM status and exercise caution when transiting, but entry is not prohibited.

Examples of Class F(A):
- Parachute jumping areas (CYA designations)
- Soaring/glider activity areas
- Military training areas (when active but advisory)
- Bird hazard areas near wildlife refuges

**Memory hook:** F(R) = Restricted = you need permission. F(A) = Advisory = you are warned but not blocked. Think of the "(A)" as "Advice only."

---

### Designation Prefixes in Canadian Special Airspace

Canadian special use airspace uses a system of letter-number designations:

| Prefix | Type |
|--------|------|
| **CYR** | Restricted area |
| **CYA** | Advisory area |
| **CYD** | Danger area |
| **CYT** | Training area (typically military) |
| **CYM** | Military operations area |

These designations appear on VFR charts (VNC) and in NOTAMs. When you see a CYR on your chart, check the chart legend for the controlling authority and frequency, then check NOTAMs for active hours and authorization requirements.

---

### Military Operations Areas (MOAs)

Military Operations Areas are volumes of airspace (often Class F) where military training exercises take place — formation flying, tactical manoeuvres, simulated combat. MOAs may be active at specific times published by NOTAM.

VFR aircraft technically may transit a MOA that is Class F(A) — it is advisory, not restricted — but it is strongly inadvisable when military activity is occurring. The combination of fast-moving military aircraft, tight formations, and low-altitude maneuvering makes VFR penetration extremely dangerous. Contact the controlling authority (usually military radar) before transiting an active MOA.

---

### Air Defence Identification Zone (ADIZ)

The ADIZ is one of the most important special airspace concepts for Canadian pilots, particularly those operating near the coasts or the US border.

**What is the ADIZ?**

Canada's ADIZ is a large buffer zone extending around Canadian airspace, particularly along the southern border with the United States and the coastal approaches. Its purpose is to give NORAD (North American Aerospace Defense Command) early warning of unidentified aircraft approaching Canadian airspace.

**DVFR Flight Plans**

Any aircraft intending to enter the Canadian ADIZ under VFR must file a **DVFR flight plan** (Defence VFR flight plan) before departure. This is filed with a NAV CANADA flight information centre just like a regular VFR flight plan.

The DVFR flight plan must include an estimated time of entry into the ADIZ. NORAD and ATC use this to distinguish identified traffic from potentially unidentified intruders. Failure to file a DVFR flight plan before entering the ADIZ can trigger a military intercept.

IFR flights also require identification procedures, typically through the IFR flight plan and ATC coordination.

**Key requirement: DVFR flight plan filed before departure, not just before entry.**

---

### Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)

TFRs are temporary volumes of restricted airspace established by NOTAM for specific, time-limited purposes. They are not permanent on charts — you find them only in current NOTAMs.

Common reasons for TFRs in Canada:

1. **Aerial firefighting operations** — the most safety-critical TFR type. Air tankers operate at low level in smoke-obscured conditions. Unauthorized VFR aircraft entering a firefighting TFR have caused mid-air collision hazards and forced tankers to abort drops. These TFRs are treated very seriously.

2. **VIP movements** — when heads of state or other protected persons are travelling, airspace around their route and destination may be temporarily restricted.

3. **Emergency operations** — search and rescue, disaster response, major accident investigation.

4. **Special events** — major public events in controlled airspace.

**Pre-flight action:** Always check current NOTAMs for your planned route before every flight. Active TFRs are not charted — they exist only in NOTAMs. An active TFR in your path with no NOTAM check is a violation waiting to happen.

---

### Prohibited Areas

Canada also uses **Prohibited Areas** (CYP designations) for areas where flight is absolutely forbidden for national security or similar reasons — for example, airspace over certain government facilities. Prohibited areas are permanent and do not require NOTAM activation. Entry is never authorized under normal circumstances.

---

### Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs)

CFAs are designated areas where hazardous activities (such as artillery firing) take place. Unlike Restricted areas, CFA activities are automatically suspended when an aircraft is detected approaching or entering. The hazard suspends for the aircraft, not the other way around. Pilots need not avoid CFAs — the military suspends activity automatically.

---

### How to Find Special Airspace Information

- **VFR Navigation Charts (VNC):** Class F areas are depicted with hatched boundaries and CYR/CYA designations
- **Canada Flight Supplement (CFS):** Contains details on controlling authorities, frequencies, and operating hours
- **NOTAMs:** Active TFRs, NOTAM-activated Class F(R) areas, and temporary restrictions
- **Nav Canada's Flight Planning Tools:** Current NOTAMs, pilot briefings

**Always check the CFS and NOTAMs before flight, not just the chart.** Charts are updated infrequently — NOTAMs are the source of truth for current status.

---

## Key Points

- **Class F(R):** Restricted — requires authorization from controlling authority; designated CYR
- **Class F(A):** Advisory — warns of hazards, no authorization required; exercise caution
- ADIZ entry under VFR requires a **DVFR flight plan filed before departure**
- TFRs exist only in NOTAMs — always check NOTAMs pre-flight; not shown on charts
- Aerial firefighting TFRs are safety-critical — unauthorized entry has caused incidents
- Prohibited areas (CYP) are permanently closed; no authorization under normal circumstances
- CFA activities automatically suspend when aircraft are detected — pilots need not avoid them
