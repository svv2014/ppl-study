# Lesson AL-003: VFR Weather Minimums

**Section:** Air Law  
**Lesson number:** 003  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** CARs 602.114–602.117, TP 12880E Chapter 7, AIM RAC 2.0

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson AL-003. Today we tackle one of the most exam-heavy topics in Air Law: VFR weather minimums. These are the legal minimum visibility and cloud clearance requirements that allow you to fly under Visual Flight Rules. Know these cold — they show up on almost every PPL written exam.

---

**Why Weather Minimums Exist**

VFR flight depends on the pilot being able to see where they're going — and see other aircraft. When visibility drops or clouds close in, the risk of collision and controlled flight into terrain rises sharply. Weather minimums set a floor below which VFR flight is not legal. Below those minimums, you need an IFR clearance and the rating to go with it.

The minimums vary depending on two things: the class of airspace you're flying in, and whether it's day or night.

---

**The Two Numbers You Always Need**

Every weather minimum has two components. First: flight visibility — how far you can see forward along your intended path, measured in statute miles. Second: distance from cloud — how far you must stay from clouds in three directions: below, above, and horizontally.

Remember: clouds are where IFR aircraft may be operating. The cloud clearance distances give you enough space to see and avoid an aircraft emerging from cloud.

---

**Controlled Airspace — Classes C, D, and E**

In controlled airspace during the day, the VFR minimums are the same across Classes C, D, and E:

Flight visibility: 3 statute miles.
Below cloud: 500 feet.
Above cloud: 1,000 feet.
Horizontal from cloud: 2,000 feet.

This is the standard daytime controlled airspace formula: 3 miles, 500-1000-2000. Memorize it exactly.

At night in Class C, D, and E, the visibility requirement goes up to 5 statute miles. The cloud clearance distances stay the same — 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal.

---

**Uncontrolled Airspace — Class G**

Class G has lower minimums than controlled airspace, but there's a split based on altitude.

At and below 1,000 feet AGL in Class G — day:
Visibility: 2 statute miles. Cloud clearance: clear of cloud. That's it. No separation distances. Just stay out of the cloud and see where you're going.

Above 1,000 feet AGL in Class G — day:
Visibility: 1 statute mile. Cloud clearance: 500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, 2,000 feet horizontal. Same distances as controlled airspace, but lower visibility.

Wait — did I say 1 mile visibility above 1,000 feet AGL in Class G? Yes. That's lower than controlled airspace. Class G day minimums above 1,000 feet AGL: 1 mile visibility, 500-1000-2000 cloud clearance.

At night in Class G: the rules tighten. The visibility goes to 3 statute miles. Cloud clearance stays 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal. This applies both below and above 1,000 feet AGL at night in Class G.

---

**Special VFR**

There's one more concept the exam likes: Special VFR, or SVFR. This is a clearance you can request from ATC to operate in Class C or D airspace when the weather is below the standard VFR minimums but above certain lower thresholds. Under SVFR, you need a clearance, you must remain clear of cloud, and visibility must be at least 1 statute mile. SVFR is only available in certain airspace and only when authorized by ATC — you can't just decide to fly SVFR on your own.

---

**The Memory Framework**

Here's how to keep this straight in your head. Think of it in tiers.

Tier one — controlled airspace day: 3 miles, 500-1000-2000. This is the baseline.

Tier two — controlled airspace night: visibility goes to 5 miles. Cloud clearance stays the same.

Tier three — Class G below 1,000 AGL day: 2 miles, clear of cloud. Simple and loose.

Tier four — Class G above 1,000 AGL day: 1 mile, 500-1000-2000. Surprising — lower visibility than controlled but same cloud clearance distances.

Tier five — Class G night: 3 miles, 500-1000-2000. Same cloud clearance, visibility matches controlled day.

---

**A Common Exam Trap**

Students often assume that higher visibility is always required at higher altitudes. Not quite. In Class G above 1,000 feet AGL during the day, you only need 1 mile of visibility. That's less than the 3 miles required in controlled airspace at the same altitude. The lower minimums in Class G reflect the absence of IFR traffic — no ATC separation is happening, so the rules rely on you seeing and avoiding on your own. You need enough visibility to do that — but the legal floor is lower than you might expect.

---

**Putting It On the Exam**

When a weather minimums question appears, first identify the airspace class and whether it's day or night. Then apply the correct set of numbers. The question might give you a weather scenario — two miles visibility, scattered cloud at 800 feet — and ask whether VFR flight is legal in Class D airspace. Work through it: Class D is controlled, day minimum is 3 miles visibility. Two miles is below that. Answer: not legal.

Or: you're flying at 800 feet AGL in Class G at noon. Visibility is 1.5 miles, sky is clear. Is VFR legal? You're below 1,000 feet AGL in Class G day — minimum is 2 miles visibility, clear of cloud. 1.5 miles is below the minimum. Answer: not legal.

These scenarios are the bread and butter of the weather minimums questions. Work through the airspace class, altitude, day or night, then apply the numbers.
