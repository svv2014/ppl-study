# Lesson AL-014: Emergency Procedures (Legal) — Distress and Urgency

**Section:** Air Law  
**Lesson number:** 014  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** CARs 602.135, AIM COM 5.0, TP 12880E Chapter 11

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson AL-014. In this lesson we cover the legal and procedural side of in-flight emergencies — specifically the two levels of emergency declaration recognized in Canada, the radio calls you must know, the transponder codes, and what your obligations are under the CARs after an emergency.

---

### Two Levels of Emergency

Canadian regulations and ICAO standards recognize two distinct levels of emergency, each with its own radio call and response expectation.

**Distress — MAYDAY**

A distress situation is one where an aircraft or person is threatened by a grave or imminent danger and requires immediate assistance. The word used is **MAYDAY**, spoken three times: *MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY*. This is the highest level emergency. Think: the threat is immediate and life is at stake right now. Examples include engine failure over unsuitable terrain, fire aboard the aircraft, pilot incapacitation, or fuel exhaustion with no runway in range.

The MAYDAY call format is:
1. MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY
2. Station being called (ATC facility or "ALL STATIONS")
3. Aircraft callsign
4. Nature of the emergency
5. Pilot intentions
6. Current position and altitude
7. Souls on board and fuel remaining

You don't need to recite every element if time doesn't permit — the critical thing is to get the MAYDAY out and state the nature of the problem.

**Urgency — PAN-PAN**

A PAN-PAN situation is one that concerns the safety of an aircraft or person but does not require immediate assistance. The word used is **PAN-PAN**, spoken three times: *PAN-PAN PAN-PAN PAN-PAN*. This is a serious situation — a medical event where the pilot is still in control, a fuel state that is deteriorating but not yet critical, an aircraft system problem that may worsen. You're asking for priority handling and alerting ATC, but you're not declaring imminent disaster.

---

### Transponder Codes in Emergencies

You need to know three emergency transponder codes cold:

| Code | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **7700** | General emergency (distress) |
| **7600** | Radio/communications failure |
| **7500** | Unlawful interference (hijacking) |

If you declare a MAYDAY, squawk 7700. If you lose your radio and cannot communicate, squawk 7600. These codes activate alerts at ATC radar displays and give controllers immediate awareness even without radio contact.

**Important:** If you are already squawking an assigned code when an emergency occurs, continue on that code — ATC is already tracking you. Switch to 7700 only if it will improve the situation (e.g., you lose radar contact or are changing frequencies).

---

### The Distress Frequency — 121.5 MHz

121.5 MHz is the international emergency frequency, monitored by:
- All ATC facilities
- Many commercial and military aircraft
- Emergency position-indicating radio beacons (ELTs)

If you lose contact with ATC and need to declare an emergency, 121.5 MHz is the backup channel. However, if you are already talking to ATC, transmit on the frequency currently in use — do not switch away to 121.5 first. ATC already knows your position. Switching to 121.5 costs time.

---

### Emergency Authority to Deviate

CARs 602.135 gives a pilot-in-command the authority to deviate from any CARs regulation to the extent necessary to meet an emergency that requires immediate action. This means: if following a regulation would make the situation worse, you may break that rule to save the aircraft and lives aboard.

After the emergency, if the Minister requires a written explanation of any deviation, you must provide it. This is a conditional requirement — it is triggered if the Minister asks, not automatically.

This is not a blank cheque for recklessness. The deviation must be necessary to address the emergency, and you remain the PIC responsible for the decisions made.

---

### After Landing — Reporting Obligations

Following an emergency, your reporting obligations under the CARs depend on what happened:

- **Accident:** Any occurrence where persons are injured or the aircraft sustains damage must be reported to the Transportation Safety Board (TSB) immediately.
- **Serious Incident:** Reportable to TSB.
- **Deviation from CARs:** If the Minister requests a written report, you must submit one.

The key distinction for the exam: simply declaring MAYDAY and landing safely does not by itself trigger a mandatory written report — but if the Minister requests one, you must comply. Accidents (injury or damage) are separately reportable to the TSB regardless.

---

### ELTs — Emergency Locator Transmitters

Most Canadian-registered aircraft are required to carry an ELT (Emergency Locator Transmitter) under CARs 605.38. The ELT broadcasts on 406 MHz (digital, detected by satellite) and on 121.5 MHz (for direction-finding). When the ELT activates, it transmits the aircraft's registration data to the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system, allowing Search and Rescue to locate the aircraft.

**Exam note:** ELTs transmit on **406 MHz** for satellite detection and **121.5 MHz** as the homing signal. The satellite system is Cospas-Sarsat. Source: CARs 605.38, AIM SAR.

---

## Key Points

- **MAYDAY** = distress; immediate, life-threatening danger; spoken three times
- **PAN-PAN** = urgency; serious but not immediately life-threatening; spoken three times
- Emergency transponder codes: 7700 (emergency), 7600 (radio failure), 7500 (hijacking)
- Transmit emergency call on current ATC frequency first; switch to 121.5 MHz if unable to reach ATC
- CARs 602.135 authorizes deviation from regulations in a genuine emergency
- Post-emergency written report to the Minister is conditional on request, not automatic
- ELTs transmit on 406 MHz (satellite) and 121.5 MHz (homing); required under CARs 605.38
