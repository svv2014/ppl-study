# Lesson AL-001: Canadian Airspace Classifications

**Section:** Air Law  
**Lesson number:** 001  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** CARs Part VI (Airspace), TP 12880E Chapter 7, Transport Canada AIM RAC 2.0

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to your first Air Law lesson. This one is foundational — airspace classifications come up constantly on the written exam, and understanding them will make every other Air Law topic click faster.

---

Canada divides its airspace into lettered classes, A through G. Each class has different rules about who needs clearances, what weather minimums apply, and who's providing services. Let's walk through each one.

**Class A** — Think of Class A as the high highway. In Canada, Class A airspace runs from 18,000 feet ASL — that's above sea level — up to 60,000 feet. ASL means the altitude measured from sea level, not from the ground. At these altitudes, all aircraft must be operating under Instrument Flight Rules, or IFR. You cannot fly VFR in Class A. An ATC clearance is required to enter. You won't be operating here as a PPL student, but you need to know the definition.

**Class B** — Class B in Canada is a specific layer: 12,500 feet ASL up to — but not including — 18,000 feet ASL. Like Class A, you need ATC clearance and must be IFR. VFR flight is not permitted. Class B separates lower traffic from the Class A high structure above it.

**Class C** — Now things get more interesting for VFR pilots. Class C airspace surrounds the busiest airports — airports like Toronto Pearson, Vancouver, and Montréal-Trudeau. It typically extends from the surface or from 700 feet AGL up to a defined ceiling, often around 3,000 to 4,000 feet AGL, then expands outward in a wider ring up to higher altitudes. To enter Class C airspace, a VFR pilot needs ATC clearance and must maintain radio contact. The controller provides traffic separation between IFR aircraft, and traffic advisories to VFR aircraft. Weather minimums in Class C during the day: 3 statute miles visibility, 500 feet below clouds, 1,000 feet above clouds, and 2,000 feet horizontally from clouds. Remember that formula — it shows up on the exam.

**Class D** — Class D surrounds busier airports that don't qualify for Class C — airports with control towers but lower traffic volumes. Think of mid-size airports. To enter Class D, you need to establish two-way radio contact with ATC — you don't necessarily need a clearance, but you must make contact and get a response before entering. ATC provides traffic information but does not guarantee separation from VFR traffic. Same weather minimums as Class C apply during the day.

**Class E** — Class E is controlled airspace where ATC services are available for IFR aircraft, but VFR aircraft can fly without a clearance and without talking to anyone, as long as they meet the weather minimums. Class E often exists around smaller airports without towers, along airways, or in transition areas. VFR weather minimums in Class E: 3 miles visibility, 500 below / 1,000 above / 2,000 horizontal from clouds. Same as Class C and D. The pattern here: in all controlled airspace (Class C, D, and E), the VFR weather minimums are identical during the day.

**Class F** — Class F is special use airspace. It includes areas that are restricted or advisory in nature. Class F(R) is restricted — entry requires specific authorization. Class F(A) is advisory — you don't need a clearance but you're advised of hazards. Military operations, aerobatics, parachute drop zones — these are often Class F. NOTAMs will tell you when Class F areas are active.

**Class G** — Class G is uncontrolled airspace. No ATC service, no clearance required. It's the "wild west" of airspace — VFR pilots can fly here as long as they meet the minimum weather requirements. At and below 1,000 feet AGL, the VFR minimums drop: 2 miles visibility, clear of cloud (just stay clear — no separation distances required). Above 1,000 feet AGL in Class G: 1 mile visibility and clear of clouds during the day, but during the day these minimums relax compared to controlled airspace. Know this: Class G weather minimums are lower than Class C/D/E minimums.

---

**The Memory Hook:** Classes A and B — IFR only, ATC clearance required. Class C — VFR allowed with clearance, radio contact mandatory. Class D — two-way contact required, no formal clearance needed. Class E — VFR without clearance, just meet the weather mins. Class F — special use, check NOTAMs. Class G — uncontrolled, lower weather mins apply.

---

**One more concept before we close:** The distinction between "ASL" and "AGL." ASL means Above Sea Level — a fixed reference that doesn't change with terrain. AGL means Above Ground Level — the height above whatever ground is directly below you. Class A starts at 18,000 feet ASL. That's 18,000 feet above sea level whether you're over the Prairies or over the Rockies. AGL changes depending on the terrain.

That's the airspace framework. Review the chart below after this lesson — visualizing the layers helps solidify the vertical structure.
