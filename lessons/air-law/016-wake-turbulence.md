# Lesson AL-016: Wake Turbulence — Categories, Avoidance Rules, Timing

**Section:** Air Law  
**Lesson number:** 016

## Narration Script

Welcome to Lesson AL-016. Wake turbulence is one of the most dangerous invisible hazards in aviation. Understanding what it is, where it goes, and how to avoid it is both an exam topic and a practical safety skill you'll use every time you fly near larger aircraft.

---

### What Is Wake Turbulence?

When an aircraft flies, it generates lift by creating a pressure differential between the upper and lower surfaces of the wing. Air spills around the wingtips from the high-pressure region below to the low-pressure region above, forming two counter-rotating vortices — one trailing behind each wingtip.

These vortices are invisible, can extend hundreds of feet, rotate at very high angular velocities, and can generate forces that exceed the roll control authority of a light aircraft. An encounter with a vortex from a heavy jet can instantly roll a light aircraft inverted.

The strength of the vortex depends on:
- **Aircraft weight** — heavier aircraft generate stronger vortices
- **Speed** — slower aircraft generate stronger vortices (more lift needed at lower speed)
- **Wing configuration** — flaps extended (landing config) generates intense vortices at low speed

The worst case for wake turbulence encounter: **a heavy jet, slow speed, flaps extended, on approach** — maximum vortex strength, low altitude, little time to recover.

---

### ICAO Wake Turbulence Categories

Aircraft are classified into three wake turbulence categories based on maximum certificated take-off weight (MCTOW):

| Category | MCTOW |
|----------|-------|
| **Heavy (H)** | 136,000 kg or more (e.g., B747, A380) |
| **Medium (M)** | More than 7,000 kg and less than 136,000 kg (e.g., B737, Dash 8) |
| **Light (L)** | 7,000 kg or less (e.g., Cessna 172, Piper Archer) |

**Super Heavy** — ICAO has added a "Super" category for aircraft like the Airbus A380, which generates particularly intense vortices. ATC separation applies increased spacing behind Super aircraft.

For the Canadian PPL exam, know Heavy, Medium, and Light, and the weight cutoffs.

---

### Vortex Behaviour

**Where vortices form:** Vortices begin at the point of rotation on takeoff and end at the point of touchdown on landing. They trail behind the aircraft and slightly below its flight path.

**Sink rate:** Vortices sink at approximately 300–500 feet per minute. They level off at around 500–900 feet below the generating aircraft's altitude.

**Drift:** In a crosswind, both vortices drift downwind. A light crosswind (around 3 knots) can hold the upwind vortex near the runway centreline while pushing the downwind vortex further out.

**Persistence:** In calm, stable air, vortices persist for 3–4 minutes and can remain hazardous even longer. Turbulence and stronger winds break them up faster.

**Ground effect:** Near the ground, vortices bounce off the surface and actually move outward from the centreline at about 5 knots. This means on a calm day, a vortex can hover near the runway surface and drift toward the edge — not dissipate.

---

### Avoidance — Landing

When **landing behind a heavier aircraft on the same runway:**

1. Note the touchdown point of the preceding aircraft — vortices exist from that point back.
2. Stay above the preceding aircraft's approach path.
3. Plan to touch down beyond the point where the preceding aircraft touched down.
4. If unable to maintain the separation, go around.

ATC separation for a light aircraft landing behind a heavy aircraft on the same runway is **2 minutes**. You may request additional separation.

If the preceding aircraft executed a touch-and-go, the vortex from the rollout and departure is still present. Apply the same avoidance principles.

---

### Avoidance — Takeoff

When **departing behind a heavier aircraft:**

1. Note the rotation point of the preceding aircraft — vortices begin there.
2. Plan to rotate at or before the preceding aircraft's rotation point.
3. Climb above and upwind of the preceding departure flight path.
4. ATC applies 2-minute separation — do not take off until separation is applied.

If the preceding aircraft departed the same runway in the same direction, the vortices trail along its climb path. Staying above that path keeps you clear.

If the preceding aircraft took off on an intersecting runway, be aware that their vortex may cross your path if the flight paths converge.

---

### Special Situations

**Landing opposite direction on the same runway (opposite-direction operations):** If a heavy aircraft just landed toward you and you are departing or landing in the opposite direction, its vortices are sinking and drifting from its touchdown point. Avoid flying through the altitude where vortices were generated.

**Low passes:** A heavy aircraft conducting a low pass above a runway leaves vortices at low altitude that sink to the runway surface. Avoid operations on that runway for at least 3 minutes.

**Parallel runways:** Vortex drift in a crosswind can carry vortices from one runway to a parallel runway. If a crosswind is blowing from the parallel runway, be cautious about vortex penetration.

---

### Pilot-in-Command Responsibility

ATC provides wake turbulence separation as a service, but when ATC issues a clearance with the phrase "caution wake turbulence" or when you are operating VFR without ATC separation, the responsibility for wake turbulence avoidance rests entirely with you as pilot-in-command.

If ATC spacing seems insufficient, you have the right — and the responsibility — to request additional separation or to delay your takeoff or landing to ensure safety. Do not accept a clearance that places you in a wake turbulence risk if you believe the situation is unsafe.

---

## Key Points

- Wake vortices form at the wingtips of aircraft generating lift; strongest behind heavy, slow, landing-configured aircraft
- Categories: **Heavy** (≥136,000 kg), **Medium** (7,000–136,000 kg), **Light** (≤7,000 kg)
- Vortices sink 300–500 ft/min and drift downwind; persist 3–4 min in calm stable air
- On landing behind heavy: stay above their approach path; touch down beyond their touchdown point; 2-minute ATC separation applies
- On takeoff behind heavy: rotate at or before their rotation point; climb above their departure path
- Vortices start at rotation (takeoff) and end at touchdown (landing)
- PIC is responsible for wake turbulence avoidance even when ATC provides separation service
