# Lesson GK-011: Takeoff and Landing Performance

**Section:** General Knowledge  
**Lesson number:** 011  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 11, POH (aircraft-specific)

---

## Narration Script

Takeoff and landing accidents account for a significant proportion of all general aviation incidents. Most are not caused by mechanical failure — they result from mismanagement of the available runway, misjudging environmental conditions, or attempting to salvage an unstabilized approach. This lesson covers the climb speeds, runway definitions, and the factors that most affect takeoff and landing performance.

---

**Climb Speed Definitions**

After liftoff, two specific airspeeds govern climb performance:

**Vx — Best Angle of Climb**

Vx gives the greatest altitude gain per unit of horizontal distance. It is used to **clear obstacles** immediately after takeoff — climbing steeply over trees, power lines, or terrain at the end of the runway. Vx corresponds to the speed at which excess thrust (thrust minus drag) is greatest.

**Vy — Best Rate of Climb**

Vy gives the greatest altitude gain per unit of time. It is used for **normal climb** after clearing any nearby obstacles, to reach cruise altitude efficiently. Vy corresponds to the speed at which excess power (power available minus power required) is greatest.

**Relationship**: Vx < Vy always. As altitude increases, Vx rises slightly and Vy decreases. At the **absolute ceiling**, Vx = Vy — the aircraft can only climb at one specific speed, and the rate of climb is zero. The **service ceiling** is the altitude at which maximum climb rate is 100 fpm.

**Obstacle clearance procedure**: after rotation, establish Vx to clear obstacles, then transition to Vy after clearing the obstacle.

---

**Runway Declared Distances**

Canadian airports may publish declared distances under CARs. These describe the available ground and airspace for different phases of the takeoff and landing:

**TORA** — Take-Off Run Available: the declared runway length available for the takeoff ground roll. This is the physical pavement.

**TODA** — Take-Off Distance Available: TORA plus any **clearway** (the clear area beyond the runway end, over which the aircraft may make the initial climb). The clearway must be free of obstacles to a specified height. TODA is used when the obstacle clearance portion of the takeoff can be made over the clearway.

**ASDA** — Accelerate-Stop Distance Available: TORA plus any **stopway** (a prepared area beyond the runway end where the aircraft can be decelerated and stopped in an emergency abort). Stopways cannot be used for takeoff rolls or initial climb.

**LDA** — Landing Distance Available: the declared runway length for landing. May be shorter than TORA if a displaced threshold exists.

---

**Factors Affecting Takeoff Performance**

**1. Density Altitude**

High density altitude (high elevation, hot temperature, high humidity) reduces engine power, propeller thrust, and wing lift at a given indicated airspeed. The aircraft must accelerate to a higher true airspeed before generating sufficient lift. Takeoff roll increases substantially.

Rule of thumb: for every 1,000 ft of density altitude above sea level, takeoff distance increases approximately 10–15% (varies by aircraft — always use the POH charts).

**2. Aircraft Weight**

Heavier aircraft require a higher takeoff speed (stall speed scales with the square root of weight). More runway is needed to accelerate to that higher speed, and the climb rate is reduced.

**3. Wind**

- **Headwind**: reduces the ground speed required at liftoff. A 10-knot headwind when liftoff speed is 55 knots means you only need 45 knots of ground speed → significantly shorter roll.
- **Tailwind**: the aircraft must reach a higher ground speed before reaching flying speed → dramatically longer roll. Even a 5-knot tailwind can add 20–30% to ground roll.

Rule of thumb: a tailwind equal to 10% of liftoff speed approximately doubles the takeoff ground roll.

**4. Runway Slope**

Uphill slope increases takeoff distance (the aircraft is working against gravity during the roll). Downhill slope reduces takeoff distance. Effects are modest compared to wind and density altitude.

**5. Surface Condition**

Soft grass, gravel, or a rough surface increases rolling resistance and extends ground roll. Wet grass can be worse than expected. Paved, dry runway gives minimum ground roll.

---

**Factors Affecting Landing Performance**

**1. Approach Speed**

Landing distance is highly sensitive to approach speed. Because kinetic energy (and therefore braking distance) scales with speed squared, a 10% higher approach speed increases landing roll by approximately 21%. Fly the approach speed specified in the POH for the given weight and configuration.

**2. Wind**

- **Headwind**: reduces ground speed at touchdown → shorter roll
- **Tailwind**: increases ground speed → much longer roll

**3. Density Altitude**

Higher density altitude means a higher true airspeed at the same indicated approach speed → more kinetic energy → longer landing roll.

**4. Runway Surface**

- **Dry paved**: best braking — baseline for POH charts
- **Wet paved**: braking effectiveness reduced significantly; expect doubled ground roll or more
- **Contaminated (ice, slush, snow)**: braking nearly absent in severe cases; add very large margins

**5. Slope**

Downhill landing increases stopping distance. Uphill landing decreases it (but can complicate the approach and go-around).

---

**Stabilized Approach**

The stabilized approach concept is fundamental to safe landings. A stabilized approach requires that by a defined point (typically 500 ft AGL in VMC for light aircraft):

- The aircraft is at the correct airspeed (±5 knots)
- The aircraft is on the correct glidepath
- The aircraft is in the correct configuration (flaps, gear)
- Power is set and stable
- The aircraft is aligned with the runway centreline

If any of these criteria are not met, **go around**. Most runway excursions result from pilots attempting to salvage an unstabilized approach rather than executing a go-around.

---

**Short-Field and Soft-Field Techniques**

**Short-field takeoff**: apply full power before releasing brakes; rotate at the exact POH rotation speed; establish Vx immediately to clear obstacles. Lean the mixture for maximum power at high-elevation airports.

**Soft-field takeoff**: apply full power and keep moving (don't allow the aircraft to stop in the soft surface); raise the nosewheel early to reduce main-gear resistance; fly in ground effect initially to accelerate to Vy before climbing away.

**Short-field landing**: maintain precise approach speed (typically 1.3 × Vs0); cross the threshold at 50 ft; apply brakes firmly after touchdown; raise flaps to transfer weight to the wheels for maximum braking.

---

## Key Points

- **Vx** (best angle): clear obstacles; **Vy** (best rate): normal climb; Vx < Vy
- At absolute ceiling: Vx = Vy and climb rate = 0; service ceiling = 100 fpm climb rate
- **TODA** = TORA + clearway; **ASDA** = TORA + stopway
- **Tailwind** dramatically increases takeoff and landing distances — avoid when marginal
- High density altitude, high weight, tailwind, upslope → longest takeoff distances
- **Landing distance**: scales with speed² — maintain the correct approach speed
- Wet/icy runways greatly exceed POH (dry) chart distances
- **Stabilized approach**: go around if not stable by 500 ft AGL in VMC
