# Lesson GK-004: Fuel System

**Section:** General Knowledge  
**Lesson number:** 004  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 5, CARs 602.88, POH (aircraft-specific)

---

## Narration Script

Fuel management is one of the most basic pilot responsibilities — yet fuel exhaustion (running out of fuel) remains one of the most preventable causes of aircraft accidents. This lesson covers fuel types, fuel system components, pre-flight checks, and the legal reserve requirements under Canadian regulations.

---

**Aviation Fuel Types**

**Avgas (Aviation Gasoline)** — used in piston aircraft engines:

- **100LL (100 Low Lead)**: the standard avgas in Canada and most of the world. Dyed **blue**. The "100" refers to the octane rating. "LL" means low lead — still contains some tetraethyl lead as a detonation inhibitor, but less than older grades.
- **80/87**: an older, lower-octane grade. Dyed **red**. Rarely found today.
- **100 (not LL)**: dyed **green**. Higher lead content, less common.

**Jet Fuel (Aviation Turbine Fuel)** — used in turbine (jet and turboprop) engines:

- **Jet-A**: the most common turbine fuel. Clear to straw-coloured.
- **Jet-A1**: similar to Jet-A with a lower freezing point.
- **Jet B**: wide-cut fuel used in cold climates. Dyed red-orange.

**Misfuelling danger**: Jet fuel in a piston engine will not ignite properly — engine failure will result. Avgas in a turbine engine causes combustion chamber and turbine damage. Fuel nozzle sizes and shapes are designed to prevent misfuelling, but always verify fuel type before accepting fuel.

---

**Fuel System Components**

A typical light aircraft fuel system includes:

1. **Fuel tanks**: usually in the wings; may be integral (sealed wing structure) or bladder tanks.
2. **Fuel vents**: allow air to replace fuel as it is consumed; prevent vacuum lock. Vent blockage (ice, insects) can stop fuel flow.
3. **Fuel selector valve**: allows the pilot to select which tank feeds the engine. Common positions: LEFT, RIGHT, BOTH, OFF.
4. **Fuel strainer (gascolator)**: a filter at the lowest point of the system that traps sediment and water. Must be drained during pre-flight.
5. **Fuel gauge**: indicates fuel quantity. Gauges are only required to be accurate at empty — always visually check fuel level during pre-flight.
6. **Primer**: injects fuel directly into the intake manifold for cold starts.

---

**Gravity vs. Pump Feed**

**High-wing aircraft** (e.g., Cessna 172): fuel tanks are above the engine; gravity feeds fuel without a pump. Simple and reliable. Carbureted high-wing aircraft typically have a fuel selector and gravity flow.

**Low-wing aircraft** (e.g., Piper Cherokee): fuel tanks are below the engine; an engine-driven fuel pump is required. A backup **electric boost pump** provides fuel pressure during start, takeoff, and landing in case of engine pump failure.

---

**Pre-Flight Fuel Check**

Before every flight:

1. **Visually inspect tanks**: physically check (by looking into the tank filler neck) to verify quantity. Gauges are unreliable when not at zero.
2. **Sump drains**: drain fuel from each tank sump and the fuel strainer. Check for:
   - **Water**: appears as separate drops (water is clear, heavier than blue avgas) or a distinct layer at the bottom of the sample cup.
   - **Sediment**: particles in the fuel sample.
   - **Correct colour**: blue for 100LL.
3. **Fuel cap security**: ensure fuel caps are properly seated to prevent in-flight fuel loss or contamination.
4. **Vent clear**: check fuel vent openings are free from ice or insects.

---

**Fuel Reserve Requirements — CARs 602.88**

Under Canadian regulations, a pilot must carry enough fuel to:

1. Fly to the destination
2. Fly to an alternate aerodrome, if an alternate is required
3. **Plus a reserve of 30 minutes at normal cruise power (VFR day)**
4. **Plus a reserve of 45 minutes at normal cruise power (VFR night)**

These are minimums — conservative pilots carry more. Running low on fuel forces decisions that compromise safety; running out of fuel is always a pilot error.

---

**Fuel Management in Flight**

- Use the recommended fuel tank sequencing from the POH.
- Many aircraft with two-tank systems specify "BOTH" for takeoff and landing; some specify individual tanks — follow the POH.
- Switch tanks before they run dry — running a tank to empty can cause fuel starvation before the selector is switched.
- Monitor fuel gauges and calculated consumption against planned consumption.
- **Fuel exhaustion vs. fuel starvation**: exhaustion = tanks are empty; starvation = fuel is available but not reaching the engine (wrong selector, blocked vent, failed pump).

---

**Vapour Lock**

At high temperatures, avgas can vaporize in the fuel lines. Bubbles of vapour block liquid fuel flow — the engine loses power or stops. Risk is highest on hot days or after a hot engine soak. The electric boost pump pressurizes the fuel lines and prevents vapour lock — always check boost pump on in hot conditions during pre-takeoff checks.

---

## Key Points

- **100LL avgas**: blue, used in piston engines; Jet-A is clear/straw, used in turbines
- **Misfuelling**: jet fuel in piston engine = engine failure; avgas in turbine = damage
- **Sump drains**: check for water (separate drops), sediment, and correct colour
- **Fuel gauges** are only required accurate at empty — always visually check tanks
- **VFR day reserve**: 30 minutes at normal cruise power (CARs 602.88)
- **VFR night reserve**: 45 minutes at normal cruise power (CARs 602.88)
- **Gravity feed** (high-wing): simpler, no pump needed; **pump feed** (low-wing): electric boost pump critical on takeoff and landing
- Vapour lock: fuel vaporizes in hot lines; boost pump prevents it
