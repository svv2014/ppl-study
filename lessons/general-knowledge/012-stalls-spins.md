# Lesson GK-012: Stalls and Spins

**Section:** General Knowledge  
**Lesson number:** 012  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapters 3, 12

---

## Narration Script

Stalls and spins account for a significant proportion of fatal general aviation accidents, most occurring during the approach to land or during turns near the ground. Understanding what causes a stall, how stall speed changes with bank angle and load, and how to recover from a spin is critical knowledge for any pilot.

---

**The Stall — A Review**

A stall is an aerodynamic event: the wing exceeds its critical angle of attack (AOA). When the AOA goes beyond approximately 15–18 degrees (depending on the aerofoil), airflow can no longer follow the curved upper surface. It separates, creating turbulent wake behind the wing. Lift drops sharply and drag increases.

**Key point**: stall is about AOA, not airspeed. An aircraft can stall:
- At low speed in level flight (the "classic" stall)
- At high speed in a steep turn (accelerated stall)
- At any attitude, including nose-down

The stall speed published in the POH (Vs1 for clean, Vs0 for flaps extended) assumes straight and level, 1G flight at maximum weight.

---

**Load Factor and Stall Speed**

In a level banked turn, the lift must support the aircraft weight AND provide centripetal force — the total lift required exceeds the aircraft weight. This ratio is the **load factor (G)**.

Load factor in a level turn:
- 30° bank → 1.15 G
- 45° bank → 1.41 G
- 60° bank → 2.0 G
- 75° bank → 3.9 G

Stall speed increases with the square root of the load factor:

Stall speed in turn = Wings-level stall speed × √(load factor)

At 60° bank: Vs × √2 = Vs × 1.41 → 41% higher stall speed.

This is why steep turns near the ground are so dangerous. A pilot turning from base to final leg who overshoots and steepens the bank risks an accelerated stall at low altitude with insufficient room to recover.

---

**Recognizing a Stall**

Pre-stall cues:
- Mushy controls — less effective elevator and aileron
- Reduced airspeed reading (if watching the ASI)
- Stall warning horn or indicator activating (typically 5–10 knots above stall speed)
- Buffeting — turbulent airflow from the stalled portion of the wing strikes the tail

At full stall: abrupt loss of lift, nose drop (in most aircraft designed with positive stability), possible wing drop (roll) if the stall is asymmetric.

---

**Stall Recovery**

1. **Reduce AOA** — apply forward pressure (reduce back pressure or push forward). This is the non-intuitive part: when the aircraft buffets and nose drops, the instinct is to pull back. That makes it worse.
2. **Apply power** — full power to arrest altitude loss and regain speed.
3. **Level wings** — with coordinated aileron and rudder. Avoid using aileron alone — an aileron deflected down on a near-stalled wing can aggravate the stall on that wing.
4. **Recover from descent** — once flying speed is regained, pitch up gently to resume normal flight.

---

**Spin Entry**

A spin requires two conditions simultaneously:
1. **Stall** (wing at or beyond critical AOA)
2. **Yaw** (uncoordinated flight — usually from crossed controls or rudder application)

When one wing stalls before the other (due to yaw), the more deeply stalled wing generates less lift and more drag. The drag differential causes autorotation — the aircraft rotates about the vertical axis, one wing going down (stalled, inside), one wing going up (less stalled, outside).

The most common accident scenario: overshooting the base-to-final turn, the pilot tightens the bank and adds bottom rudder. The aircraft yaws, one wing stalls more deeply, and a spin begins at low altitude with no room for recovery.

---

**Spin Phases**

1. **Incipient spin**: entry into autorotation; first 1–2 turns; recoverable quickly
2. **Developed spin**: steady autorotation; constant descent rate; recovery possible with correct technique
3. **Flat spin**: rotational speed high, pitch attitude nearly level; very difficult to recover; often unrecoverable in most light aircraft

---

**Spin Recovery — PARE**

**P** — Power to idle (power can worsen rotation)
**A** — Ailerons neutral (crossed controls worsen spin)
**R** — Rudder full opposite to the direction of rotation (to stop autorotation; this is the key input)
**E** — Elevator forward/down (to reduce AOA and unstall both wings)

Once rotation stops, recover from the resulting dive smoothly to avoid a secondary stall from pulling too aggressively.

**Determine rotation direction**: look at the turn coordinator or watch which way the ground appears to rotate.

---

**Spin vs. Spiral Dive**

A spin and a spiral dive look superficially similar from inside the cockpit but are different:

| | Spin | Spiral Dive |
|---|---|---|
| Airspeed | Low, constant | High, increasing |
| Attitude | Nose down, rotating | Nose down, curving |
| AOA | Beyond critical | Normal, not stalled |
| Recovery | PARE | Reduce power, level wings, pull out |

Pulling back on the stick in a spiral dive tightens the spiral and may exceed structural limits. Reduce power and level wings first.

---

## Key Points

- **Stall** = exceeding critical AOA; can occur at any speed or attitude
- Stall speed increases with load factor: higher bank angle = higher stall speed
- At **60° bank**, stall speed is 41% higher than wings-level
- **Stall recovery**: reduce AOA (push forward), add power, level wings
- **Spin** = stall + yaw; one wing more stalled than the other → autorotation
- **Spin recovery (PARE)**: Power idle, Ailerons neutral, Rudder opposite, Elevator forward
- Spin ≠ spiral dive; do not confuse recovery procedures
