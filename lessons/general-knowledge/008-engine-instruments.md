# Lesson GK-008: Engine Instruments

**Section:** General Knowledge  
**Lesson number:** 008  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapters 5, 8; POH (aircraft-specific)

---

## Narration Script

Engine instruments give the pilot a window into what is happening inside the engine — temperatures, pressures, and speeds that cannot be seen or heard directly. Understanding what each instrument measures, what the normal ranges are, and what abnormal readings mean allows you to catch problems early and make informed decisions in flight.

---

**Tachometer**

The tachometer measures engine crankshaft rotational speed in **revolutions per minute (RPM)**. In a **fixed-pitch propeller aircraft** (such as the Cessna 172), the tachometer is the primary power control instrument — advancing the throttle increases RPM. The POH specifies:

- **Maximum RPM (red line)**: never exceed
- **Normal operating range (green arc)**: acceptable cruise RPM band
- **Takeoff RPM**: typically near maximum — specified in the POH

The tachometer also drives the **Hobbs meter** or **engine hour meter** (on some aircraft) to record engine operating time for maintenance tracking.

In a **constant-speed propeller aircraft**, the tachometer shows the RPM set by the propeller governor, while the **manifold pressure gauge** indicates the engine's power output. These two instruments are used together.

---

**Manifold Pressure Gauge (MAP)**

The manifold pressure gauge measures the pressure of the fuel-air mixture in the engine intake manifold, in **inches of mercury (in Hg)**. It is only fitted to aircraft with constant-speed propellers (where throttle position does not directly control RPM).

- At sea level with the engine stopped: MAP reads approximately 29–30 in Hg (equal to ambient atmospheric pressure)
- At idle: MAP drops to 12–15 in Hg (the throttle plate restricts airflow, creating a partial vacuum)
- At full throttle: MAP rises toward ambient atmospheric pressure

**Power setting**: power in a constant-speed propeller aircraft is set by a combination of RPM (propeller control) and manifold pressure (throttle). The POH provides approved power setting tables.

**Overboosting**: advancing the throttle too quickly or setting MAP higher than RPM permits can cause excessive cylinder pressures. Most POHs specify never to set MAP higher than RPM expressed in the same relative terms (e.g., avoid "square" power with MAP exceeding the hundreds digit of RPM).

---

**Oil Pressure Gauge**

Oil pressure is one of the most critical engine parameters. The engine-driven oil pump circulates oil to lubricate bearings, cool components, and (in constant-speed prop aircraft) operate the propeller governor.

- **Normal range**: specified in the POH (typically 25–90 psi for most light aircraft engines)
- **Minimum**: below the minimum is an emergency — engine damage begins within seconds
- **Maximum**: high pressure on a cold engine at start is normal; it should fall as oil warms

**After engine start**: oil pressure must rise to the normal operating range within approximately 30 seconds (or sooner in warm conditions). If it does not, shut down immediately. Operating without oil pressure causes rapid bearing failure.

**In-flight low oil pressure**: indicates pump failure, oil loss, or blockage. Reduce power to minimum required, divert to the nearest suitable airport, and prepare for possible engine failure. Do not ignore a low oil pressure reading.

---

**Oil Temperature Gauge**

The oil temperature gauge shows the temperature of the oil in the engine (or in the oil cooler/sump). Oil temperature is important because:

- Cold oil is thick and provides less effective lubrication at extreme cold
- Excessively hot oil loses viscosity and lubricating ability
- Oil temperature rising without reason can indicate insufficient cooling or oil loss

**Normal range**: specified in the POH (typically 75–245°F / 24–118°C for many Lycoming and Continental engines — always check the specific aircraft POH).

Allow the engine to warm to the normal oil temperature range before applying high power for takeoff — this ensures adequate lubrication. A prolonged high oil temperature in cruise suggests a cooling problem or low oil quantity.

---

**Cylinder Head Temperature (CHT)**

The CHT gauge monitors the temperature of one or more engine cylinders. It uses a thermocouple probe inserted into the cylinder head.

- **Normal range**: specified in the POH — typically 150–435°F for Lycoming engines
- **Maximum**: red line, never exceed. High CHT causes detonation and pre-ignition risk.

**Causes of high CHT:**
- Sustained high power operation (climb at full power with low airspeed = low cooling airflow)
- Mixture leaned too aggressively (excessively lean mixture burns hotter)
- Prolonged ground operation (no airflow over the cowling)
- Clogged cooling fins

**Managing CHT:**
- Enrich the mixture slightly to reduce combustion temperature
- Reduce power or lower the nose to increase airspeed (more cooling airflow)
- Allow the engine to cool before shutting down after high-power operation

---

**Exhaust Gas Temperature (EGT)**

The EGT gauge measures the temperature of exhaust gases exiting one or more cylinders. It is the primary tool for **mixture management** in cruise.

As the mixture is leaned from full rich:
1. EGT rises as the fuel-air ratio approaches stoichiometric (chemically perfect)
2. EGT reaches **peak EGT** at approximately stoichiometric mixture
3. Leaning further beyond peak causes EGT to drop again (but CHT and detonation risk increase)

**Best power mixture**: approximately 50°F rich of peak EGT — the mixture gives maximum power output while keeping temperatures safe.

**Best economy mixture**: at or slightly lean of peak EGT — maximum fuel efficiency for a given power setting. Not all aircraft manufacturers approve leaning to this setting.

Always follow the specific POH procedures for mixture management.

---

**Fuel Quantity and Flow**

**Fuel quantity gauges**: show the quantity of fuel in each tank. Under CARs, gauges are only required to be accurate at the **empty** reading — other readings may be inaccurate. Always visually inspect fuel quantity during pre-flight.

**Fuel flow gauge (fuel-injected engines)**: measures fuel flow in gallons per hour or litres per hour. Used for mixture setting and flight planning (actual vs. planned fuel burn). Compare fuel flow to the POH tables to confirm the engine is operating correctly.

---

**Ammeter / Loadmeter**

While not strictly an engine instrument, the ammeter monitors the electrical charging system, which is engine-driven.

- **Centre-zero ammeter**: positive deflection = alternator charging; negative = battery discharging (alternator may have failed)
- **Loadmeter (right-zero type)**: shows total electrical load in amps; normally reads within the green arc

An unexplained deflection to the negative (discharge) side in cruise indicates alternator failure — reduce electrical load immediately and plan to land.

---

## Key Points

- **Tachometer**: RPM; primary power control in fixed-pitch aircraft; engine-driven
- **Manifold pressure**: in Hg; used with constant-speed propellers; avoid overboosting
- **Oil pressure**: must be in normal range within ~30 s of start; low in-flight = divert immediately
- **Oil temperature**: allow warm-up before high power; high temp = cooling problem or low oil
- **CHT**: high CHT causes detonation risk; manage with mixture enrichment and airspeed
- **EGT**: peaks at stoichiometric mixture; lean to ~50°F rich of peak for best power in cruise
- **Fuel gauges**: only accurate at empty; always visually check tanks before flight
