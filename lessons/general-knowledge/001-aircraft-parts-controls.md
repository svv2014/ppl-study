# Lesson GK-001: Aircraft Parts and Controls

## Narration Script

Welcome to the General Knowledge section of your PPL study. This first lesson covers the physical structure of a light aircraft — the parts, what they do, and how the pilot controls them. These fundamentals underlie every other general knowledge topic.

---

A light training aircraft — like a Cessna 172 or a Piper Cherokee — is built from a few major structural groups: the fuselage, the wings, the empennage, the landing gear, and the powerplant. Let's work through each.

**The Fuselage** is the main body of the aircraft. It houses the cockpit, passengers, and cargo. Structurally it must be rigid enough to handle flight loads and transmit forces between the wings and tail.

**The Wings** generate lift. Each wing has a leading edge (the front), a trailing edge (the rear), a root (where it attaches to the fuselage), and a tip (the outer end). The shape of the wing cross-section is called an aerofoil or airfoil — it's designed so that air flows faster over the curved upper surface, reducing pressure and generating lift. We'll go deep on that in the aerodynamics lesson.

Attached to the trailing edge of each wing near the fuselage are the **flaps**. Flaps increase the wing's camber — its curvature — which increases lift at lower speeds and also increases drag. Pilots extend flaps on approach and landing to fly slower and steepen the descent path. Flaps are not used for roll control — that's the ailerons.

The **ailerons** are hinged panels on the outboard (outer) trailing edge of each wing. They move opposite to each other: when the left aileron goes down, the right goes up. The down aileron increases lift on that wing; the up aileron reduces lift on the other wing. The result: the aircraft rolls. Ailerons control roll around the longitudinal axis — the axis that runs nose to tail.

One effect to know: **adverse yaw**. When you roll right, the right (up-going) aileron reduces lift and drag, while the left (down-going) aileron increases both lift and induced drag. That extra drag on the left wing yaws the nose to the left — opposite to your intended turn. Pilots apply rudder in the direction of the turn to counteract adverse yaw. Some aircraft use differential aileron travel (the up aileron moves more than the down aileron) to reduce adverse yaw mechanically.

---

**The Empennage** is the tail assembly. It consists of:

- **Vertical stabilizer** (fin): a fixed surface on the tail that provides directional (yaw) stability. Like the feathers on an arrow, it keeps the nose pointed into the relative wind.
- **Rudder**: hinged to the trailing edge of the vertical stabilizer. Moving the rudder left yaws the nose left; right yaws it right. Rudder controls yaw around the vertical axis. Note: rudder does not bank the aircraft directly — it yaws it.
- **Horizontal stabilizer**: a fixed horizontal surface providing pitch (nose up/down) stability.
- **Elevator**: hinged to the trailing edge of the horizontal stabilizer. Back pressure (pulling) raises the elevator, raises the nose. Forward pressure lowers it.

Some aircraft use a **stabilator** — a single-piece horizontal tail surface that moves as a unit instead of a fixed stabilizer with a hinged elevator. The Piper Cherokee uses a stabilator.

---

**Three Axes of Flight:**

Every manoeuvre is described in terms of rotation about one of three axes:

1. **Longitudinal axis** (nose to tail) — controlled by ailerons — produces **roll**
2. **Lateral axis** (wingtip to wingtip) — controlled by elevator — produces **pitch**
3. **Vertical axis** (top to bottom) — controlled by rudder — produces **yaw**

---

**Landing Gear:**

Two main configurations exist:

- **Tricycle gear**: nosewheel forward of the main gear, main gear aft of the centre of gravity. This is the most common configuration today (Cessna 172). It's more stable on the ground and easier to land.
- **Conventional gear (tailwheel or taildragger)**: main gear forward of the CG, small tailwheel at the rear. The aircraft sits nose-high on the ground. This design is less stable directionally because the CG is behind the main gear — pilots must be more precise with rudder during takeoff and landing to prevent a **ground loop** (rapid uncontrolled yaw).

---

**Trim:**

Trim tabs are small adjustable surfaces on the trailing edge of the elevator (and sometimes rudder and ailerons). They reduce the control pressure a pilot must hold continuously. If the aircraft pitches nose-up at cruise, you apply nose-down trim to relieve that pressure. Trim does not change the aircraft's pitch — it relieves the stick force needed to maintain a pitch attitude.

---

## Key Points

- **Ailerons** → roll (longitudinal axis); always move oppositely
- **Elevator** → pitch (lateral axis)
- **Rudder** → yaw (vertical axis)
- **Flaps** → increase lift and drag; lower stall speed; used for takeoff and landing
- **Adverse yaw**: down-going aileron = more induced drag = nose yaws away from turn; correct with rudder
- **Tricycle gear**: stable, nosewheel forward; **conventional gear**: less stable, tailwheel aft
- **Trim tabs**: relieve control pressure, don't change aircraft attitude directly
