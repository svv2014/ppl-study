# Lesson GK-003: Piston Engines

**Section:** General Knowledge  
**Lesson number:** 003  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapters 5–6

---

## Narration Script

Most training aircraft use reciprocating piston engines driving a propeller — the same basic technology that powered aircraft since the Wright Flyer. Understanding how these systems work helps you manage the engine properly, identify problems early, and answer exam questions confidently.

---

**The Four-Stroke Cycle**

Most aircraft piston engines are four-stroke, spark-ignition engines. Each cylinder goes through four strokes per power cycle:

1. **Intake** — The piston moves down, drawing a fuel-air mixture through the open intake valve into the cylinder. Carburetor or fuel injector meters the fuel.

2. **Compression** — The intake valve closes. The piston moves up, compressing the mixture. This raises its temperature and pressure, making combustion more efficient.

3. **Power** — At or near top dead centre (TDC), the spark plug fires, igniting the compressed mixture. The expanding gases drive the piston down, producing power. The timing of ignition (typically 20–30 degrees before TDC) is fixed by the magneto ignition system.

4. **Exhaust** — The exhaust valve opens. The piston moves up, pushing burned gases out. Then the cycle repeats.

Memory aid: **Suck, Squeeze, Bang, Blow.**

---

**The Ignition System**

Aircraft use **magnetos** — self-contained electrical generators that produce high-voltage spark independent of the aircraft battery. Most aircraft have two magneto systems, each firing its own set of spark plugs. Dual ignition provides redundancy and improves combustion efficiency (each cylinder has two spark plugs). The magneto switch positions are LEFT, RIGHT, BOTH, and OFF. Checking each magneto individually during run-up verifies both systems work — a normal drop of 25–75 RPM when switching to one magneto is acceptable.

---

**The Carburetor and Fuel System**

The carburetor mixes fuel and air in the correct proportion before the mixture enters the cylinders. It works on the venturi principle: as air accelerates through a narrow throat, its pressure drops, drawing fuel from a float bowl.

**Carburetor ice** is a significant hazard. As fuel vaporizes and air pressure drops through the venturi, the temperature can drop 15–25°C, enough to freeze water vapour even when outside air temperature is well above 0°C. Carburetor ice is most likely between -10°C and +25°C with high humidity. It reduces power and, if severe, can stop the engine. **Carburetor heat** — a control in the cockpit — routes exhaust-heated air around the carburetor to melt or prevent ice. Applying carb heat introduces unfiltered, warm (less dense) air, which leans the mixture and typically causes a slight RPM drop.

Many modern training aircraft use fuel injection instead of carburetors. Fuel-injected engines are not susceptible to carburetor ice, but can experience vapour lock in hot conditions.

---

**The Mixture Control**

The mixture control adjusts the fuel-to-air ratio. At sea level with dense air, full rich is appropriate. As altitude increases, air density decreases — the mixture becomes progressively richer, reducing efficiency and increasing fuel consumption. Leaning the mixture (reducing fuel flow) compensates for the reduced air density. Proper leaning procedures are in the Pilot Operating Handbook (POH) for each aircraft.

---

**Propeller Basics**

The propeller converts engine torque into thrust. Each blade is an aerofoil — it generates a forward-directed aerodynamic force as it rotates.

**Fixed-pitch propellers** (most training aircraft) have a blade angle set at manufacture. You can only vary RPM with the throttle. There is no pitch control. Fixed-pitch props are simple and inexpensive.

**Constant-speed propellers** use a governor to automatically adjust blade pitch to maintain a selected RPM. At high power settings, the governor increases pitch (coarser angle) to absorb the power. This allows more efficient operation across a wider power range. The pilot sets RPM with the propeller control and power with the throttle.

---

**Propeller Effects on the Aircraft**

1. **Torque effect**: The engine torques the propeller clockwise (from behind, on most aircraft). The reaction torques the aircraft counterclockwise — a left-rolling tendency. Counteracted by design and rudder.

2. **P-factor (asymmetric blade effect)**: At high angles of attack (such as during a climb), the descending blade (right blade, viewed from behind) has a higher effective angle of attack than the ascending blade. It generates more thrust, yawing the nose left. Right rudder counteracts P-factor in a climb.

3. **Slipstream**: The rotating propwash spirals rearward around the fuselage and strikes the left side of the vertical stabilizer, creating a left-yawing tendency. Counteracted by fin offset and rudder trim.

4. **Gyroscopic precession**: The spinning propeller acts like a gyroscope. Any force applied to tilt the gyroscope results in a force 90° ahead in the direction of rotation. Significant in tailwheel aircraft during the takeoff run when the tail is raised.

---

## Key Points

- **Four strokes**: Intake → Compression → Power → Exhaust
- **Dual magnetos**: independent, battery-not-needed; run-up checks each side
- **Carburetor ice**: most likely between -10°C and +25°C with moisture; apply carb heat
- **Carb heat effect**: warms intake air, leans mixture, initial RPM drop; RPM rise confirms ice was present
- **Mixture**: lean with altitude to maintain correct fuel-air ratio
- **Fixed pitch vs. constant speed**: fixed = throttle only; constant speed = separate prop and throttle controls
- **Torque**: propeller clockwise → aircraft tends to roll left
- **P-factor**: at high AOA, descending blade generates more thrust → left yaw in climb
