# Lesson MET-005: Precipitation

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 005  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.3

---

## Narration Script

Precipitation is water in any form — liquid or solid — that falls from clouds to the surface. For pilots, precipitation matters in three ways: it reduces in-flight visibility, it can be a symptom of embedded hazards like thunderstorms, and certain types cause structural icing that can overwhelm an aircraft in minutes. This lesson covers the types of precipitation, how they form, and the hazards each presents.

---

**How Precipitation Forms**

Clouds consist of tiny water droplets or ice crystals — so small that they remain suspended. Precipitation forms when droplets or crystals grow large enough that their fall speed exceeds the upward air motion in the cloud. This happens through two main processes:

1. **Collision and coalescence** — In warm clouds (entirely above 0°C), droplets collide and merge, growing progressively larger until they fall as rain.

2. **Bergeron process (ice crystal process)** — In mixed-phase clouds (containing both ice crystals and supercooled water droplets), the vapour pressure difference causes ice crystals to grow at the expense of water droplets. The ice crystals grow, aggregate, and fall. If they pass through a warm layer, they melt into rain. This is the dominant process for precipitation in mid-latitude clouds over Canada.

---

**Types of Precipitation**

**Rain (RA)** — Liquid water droplets larger than 0.5 mm. Forms above 0°C or by melting of snow. Heavy rain reduces visibility significantly and can cause aquaplaning on runways.

**Drizzle (DZ)** — Very fine liquid droplets, smaller than 0.5 mm. Often produced by stratus clouds. Can reduce visibility to below VFR minimums even without heavy precipitation. In sub-zero temperatures, produces freezing drizzle — extremely hazardous for icing.

**Snow (SN)** — Ice crystals aggregated into flakes. Visibility in snow can drop to near zero. Snow accumulates on runways and aircraft. Wet snow is especially dangerous as it can accumulate rapidly on cold aircraft surfaces.

**Snow Grains (SG)** — Very small, white, opaque ice particles. Less common.

**Ice Pellets / Sleet (PL)** — Small, translucent pellets of ice. They form when snowflakes partially melt passing through a warm layer, then refreeze passing through a cold layer near the surface. The presence of ice pellets is a significant indicator that **freezing rain** likely exists at a slightly higher altitude where precipitation hasn't had time to refreeze. Ice pellets at the surface = check the clouds above.

**Freezing Rain (FZRA)** — Rain that falls through a surface layer below 0°C. The supercooled droplets freeze on contact with cold surfaces. This is the most hazardous precipitation type for general aviation. A thin coating of clear ice can form so rapidly that it overwhelms deicing systems. Do not attempt to fly through or beneath freezing rain in an aircraft not certified for flight in known icing.

**Freezing Drizzle (FZDZ)** — Fine drizzle at sub-freezing temperatures. Produces clear ice; dangerous despite its unimpressive name.

**Hail (GR)** — Balls or lumps of ice, larger than 5 mm, produced in intense thunderstorm updrafts. Can cause severe airframe damage. Hail can be thrown horizontally several kilometres from a thunderstorm and can fall from clear sky near the storm. **Never fly through areas of reported hail.**

**Snow Pellets (GS) / Graupel** — White, opaque, rounded ice pellets formed by riming of snowflakes. Smaller and less dense than hail.

**Virga** — Precipitation that evaporates before reaching the ground. Visible as dark streaks hanging below cloud bases. The evaporation causes cooling and sinking air — a downdraft — which can produce wind shear beneath the virga curtain. Dangerous on approach and departure.

---

**Icing Types (Precipitation-Related)**

The type of ice that forms on an aircraft depends on temperature and drop size:

| Ice Type | Conditions | Appearance | Hazard Level |
|----------|-----------|------------|--------------|
| **Clear (Glaze)** | Large droplets, T just below 0°C | Transparent, hard, smooth | Highest — changes airfoil shape |
| **Rime** | Small droplets, T –10 to –20°C | White, opaque, rough | Moderate — disturbs airflow |
| **Mixed** | Range of drop sizes, mixed phase | Combination | High — unpredictable accumulation |
| **Frost** | Sublimation on cold surface | Feathery crystals | High if on wings before takeoff |

Frost deserves special attention: even a thin layer of frost on wings before takeoff can prevent the wing from generating sufficient lift. Always de-frost aircraft prior to flight under CARs 602.11.

---

**Precipitation Intensity Descriptors**

In METARs, precipitation intensity is coded:
- No prefix — moderate (e.g., `RA` = moderate rain)
- `-` prefix — light (e.g., `-RA` = light rain)
- `+` prefix — heavy (e.g., `+TSRA` = heavy thunderstorm with rain)
- `VC` prefix — in the vicinity (within 8 km of the aerodrome) but not at the station

**Precipitation Descriptors:**
- `FZ` — Freezing (e.g., `FZRA`)
- `SH` — Showers (e.g., `SHRA`)
- `TS` — Thunderstorm (e.g., `TSRA`)
- `BL` — Blowing (e.g., `BLSN` — blowing snow)
- `DR` — Drifting (e.g., `DRSN`)

---

**Hazard Summary for Flight Planning**

When reviewing weather for a flight:
- **FZRA or FZDZ in the route forecast** — Do not depart unless the aircraft is certified for known icing (FIKI) or the freezing layer is at a safely avoidable altitude
- **Ice pellets at destination** — Expect freezing rain somewhere on the approach
- **TS anywhere on route** — Treat seriously; avoid by at least 20 nm (see MET-007)
- **Heavy SN at destination** — Verify runway condition, plan for braking action advisories
- **+RA with low visibility** — May indicate microburst activity; review winds

---

## Key Points

- **Freezing rain** (FZRA) is the most hazardous precipitation — forms clear glaze ice rapidly
- **Ice pellets** on the surface indicate freezing rain at a slightly higher altitude
- **Virga** evaporates before reaching the ground, creating downdrafts and wind shear below
- **Hail** can be thrown horizontally from a thunderstorm; never fly near reported hail
- Clear ice (large drops, near 0°C) is most aerodynamically harmful; rime ice (small drops, colder) is less harmful but still dangerous
- **Frost on wings** before takeoff must be removed — even a thin layer degrades lift (CARs 602.11)
- METAR precipitation intensity: `-` = light, no prefix = moderate, `+` = heavy
