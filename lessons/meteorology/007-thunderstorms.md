# Lesson MET-007: Thunderstorms

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 007  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.5

---

## Narration Script

Thunderstorms are the most violent and hazardous weather phenomenon general aviation pilots encounter. A single mature thunderstorm cell can generate upward vertical air currents exceeding 100 knots, hailstones the size of baseballs, lightning strikes, and microbursts that have destroyed aircraft on approach. This lesson covers thunderstorm development, hazards, and — most importantly — how to avoid them.

---

**Requirements for Thunderstorm Development**

Three ingredients are necessary for a thunderstorm:

1. **Moisture** — There must be sufficient water vapour in the atmosphere to fuel condensation and the release of latent heat that drives the storm.

2. **Lifting mechanism** — Something must trigger the initial upward motion. Common triggers: surface heating (convective), frontal lifting (frontal thunderstorms), orographic lifting (mountain thunderstorms), and upper-level divergence.

3. **Instability** — The atmosphere must be unstable so that once lifted, an air parcel continues to rise on its own. In a very stable atmosphere, lifting is suppressed; lifted parcels cool to the surrounding air temperature and sink.

---

**Thunderstorm Life Cycle**

A thunderstorm passes through three stages:

**1. Cumulus Stage (Building)**
- Dominated by rising updrafts
- Cauliflower-shaped cumulus growing rapidly upward
- Little or no precipitation at this stage
- Duration: 10–20 minutes

**2. Mature Stage (Most Dangerous)**
- The storm reaches maximum intensity
- Both strong updrafts AND downdrafts exist simultaneously and in close proximity
- Precipitation begins — heavy rain, hail, possible tornado
- Lightning is most active
- Anvil top (cirrus) spreads downwind at the tropopause
- Duration: 20–30 minutes (but new cells can keep the complex alive for hours)

**3. Dissipating Stage (Collapsing)**
- Downdrafts dominate, suppressing the updraft
- Precipitation decreases
- Cloud debris remains aloft
- Still hazardous — heavy rain, turbulence, wind shear remain

---

**Thunderstorm Hazards**

**Turbulence** — The most consistent hazard. Inside a mature Cb, turbulence can be extreme to catastrophic. Outside the cloud, clear air turbulence can extend 20+ nm from the storm. Vertical gusts of 50+ knots can exceed aircraft structural limits.

**Hail** — Hailstones form in strong updrafts. They can be thrown ahead of the storm or fall from the anvil area. Hail can be encountered in apparently clear air 10–20 nm ahead of a storm. Even small hailstones can shatter windshields and damage leading edges in seconds.

**Lightning** — Strikes can temporarily blind the pilot, damage avionics, and cause structural damage. Avoid flight in or near thunderstorms at night — lightning does not provide adequate warning, and dark adaptation is lost with each flash. If unavoidable, turn up cockpit lighting to maintain dark adaptation.

**Icing** — Severe icing from supercooled large droplets exists throughout the cloud. The mixed-phase zone (0°C to –40°C) is most dangerous.

**Microburst** — A downdraft descending from the storm base that hits the surface and spreads outward as an intense horizontal outflow. A microburst is typically 4 km in diameter and lasts less than 10 minutes but can produce wind changes of 60+ knots. On approach: initial headwind gain → core downdraft → sudden tailwind → aircraft sinks below glidepath. Has caused numerous fatal accidents worldwide.

**Tornadoes** — Rare in Canada compared to the US, but possible, especially over the Prairies. All tornadoes are associated with severe thunderstorms.

**Pressure waves** — Thunderstorms can generate atmospheric pressure waves that affect altimeter readings by 10–20 feet temporarily.

---

**Thunderstorm Types**

**Air mass thunderstorms** — Form from surface heating on hot, humid summer days. Isolated cells, usually afternoon and evening. Typically less severe than frontal storms. They tend to be visible and avoidable VFR.

**Frontal thunderstorms** — Triggered by frontal lifting. Can be embedded in cloud, making visual avoidance impossible. More severe, longer lasting, and organized in lines. A squall line ahead of a cold front can be hundreds of kilometres long.

**Orographic thunderstorms** — Triggered by lifting over mountains. Common in the Western Cordillera and along the Laurentian Highlands. Can form quickly in stable conditions aloft.

**Embedded thunderstorms** — Hidden inside nimbostratus or extensive stratiform cloud layers. No visual warning. Require airborne weather radar for detection. These are especially dangerous because pilots can inadvertently enter them while flying through seemingly unremarkable cloud.

---

**Avoidance**

The only safe strategy for light aircraft is avoidance. Specific guidelines:

- **Minimum 20 nm lateral separation** from a severe or unknown thunderstorm
- **Do not fly under the anvil** — hail falls from the anvil and turbulence beneath is severe
- **Do not fly between closely-spaced cells** — the interstitial area between storms is not safe
- **Do not attempt to outclimb a rapidly building Cb** — tops can rise 3,000 feet per minute
- **Do not attempt to fly through a line of thunderstorms** unless a radar-confirmed gap of at least 40 nm exists
- If caught near a thunderstorm, reduce airspeed to maneuvering speed (VA) to reduce structural stress from turbulence

**Navigation note:** During lightning, ADF (automatic direction finder) navigation is unreliable — lightning causes needle deflections toward the storm. GPS is unaffected.

---

**Weather Products for Thunderstorm Avoidance**

- **GFA** (Graphical Forecast for Aviation) — Shows convective activity areas
- **SIGMET** — Issued for severe or extreme turbulence associated with thunderstorms
- **Convective SIGMET** — Issued for severe thunderstorm activity
- **PIREPs** — Real-time pilot reports of turbulence and thunderstorm conditions
- **Radar** — Weather radar imagery from Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) is available online; airborne weather radar is the best real-time tool

---

## Key Points

- Thunderstorms require **moisture + instability + lifting**
- Mature stage = most hazardous: simultaneous updrafts and downdrafts, hail, lightning, icing
- **Minimum 20 nm** lateral separation from a severe thunderstorm
- **Microburst** hazard on approach: headwind gain → sudden tailwind → aircraft sinks
- **Embedded Cb** hidden in stratus/nimbostratus — cannot be avoided visually
- Do not fly under the anvil or between cells; reduce to VA if caught in turbulence
- ADF is unreliable near thunderstorms due to lightning interference
