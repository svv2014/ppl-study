# Lesson MET-011: Turbulence

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 011  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.9

---

## Narration Script

Turbulence is one of the most common weather-related complaints in aviation, but understanding its causes transforms it from a mysterious nuisance into a predictable hazard you can anticipate and manage. This lesson covers the types of turbulence, how to recognize the conditions that produce each type, the intensity scale used in PIREPs and forecasts, and the correct pilot response.

---

**What Is Turbulence**

Turbulence is irregular motion of an aircraft caused by irregular airflow — rapid, unpredictable changes in the velocity (speed and direction) of the air the aircraft is flying through. Unlike smooth, laminar airflow, turbulent air has eddies, gusts, and vertical currents that impose sudden loads on the aircraft and its occupants.

The key point for the PPL exam: turbulence does not damage well-maintained aircraft flown within their envelope at appropriate speeds. Structural damage from turbulence is almost always associated with excessive airspeed. The correct response is to slow down.

---

**Types of Turbulence**

**Convective Turbulence**

Caused by thermal activity — rising and sinking columns of air driven by unequal surface heating. As the sun heats the ground, warm air rises in thermals and cooler air sinks to replace it. Flying through this alternating up-and-down airflow produces the bumpy conditions common on hot summer afternoons.

Characteristics:
- Most intense 2–3 hours after local solar noon
- Associated with cumulus cloud development; cloud base marks the top of the thermals
- Intensity is proportional to surface temperature and instability
- Best conditions: hot, clear day over dark or dry terrain
- Smooth conditions: overcast sky (no direct solar heating), night, or stable air mass

Convective turbulence is visual — if you see rapidly building cumulus clouds, turbulence is present at and below cloud base. Flying well above cloud base (or at night before sunrise thermals develop) minimizes convective turbulence.

**Mechanical Turbulence**

Caused by friction and disruption of airflow as wind passes over uneven terrain, trees, buildings, ridges, or any surface obstruction. The mechanical disturbance creates eddies — rolling vortices — on the downwind side of obstructions.

Characteristics:
- Intensity scales directly with wind speed
- Most severe on the lee side of ridges, hills, and buildings
- Extends to a height of roughly 10× the height of the obstruction
- At low altitude on approach and landing near terrain or large buildings, can cause sudden airspeed loss or unusual attitude

Mountain wave turbulence is an extreme form of mechanical turbulence. When stable air flows over a mountain range at high speed, it can create large standing waves downwind, with severe rotor turbulence at low levels beneath the wave crests. Mountain wave turbulence can be encountered many kilometres downwind of the terrain.

**Frontal Turbulence**

Found along and near frontal boundaries, particularly cold fronts. A cold front's steep slope and rapid lifting of warm air creates vigorous vertical motion. The weather associated with cold fronts (cumulonimbus, heavy showers) accompanies severe turbulence. Warm fronts produce milder, more widespread turbulence in the extensive cloud shield ahead of the front.

**Wake Turbulence**

A specific form of mechanical turbulence created by the wingtip vortices of aircraft in flight. Vortices are strongest behind large, heavy, slow, clean-configured aircraft. A light aircraft encountering the wingtip vortex of a large jet can be rolled beyond its control authority.

Avoidance:
- Stay above and upwind of the flight path of preceding large aircraft
- ATC applies wake turbulence separation minimums between aircraft
- Most hazardous on approach behind a heavy aircraft in calm wind

**Clear Air Turbulence (CAT)**

Turbulence in the absence of cloud or other visual weather indicators. CAT is most common near jet streams — bands of high-speed wind in the upper troposphere/lower stratosphere — where strong wind shear creates invisible eddies. CAT cannot be detected by visual observation or conventional weather radar.

For PPL operations in piston aircraft at typical altitudes (below 10,000 feet ASL), CAT is less commonly encountered. However, mountain wave activity can produce CAT at moderate altitudes.

---

**Turbulence Intensity Scale**

Aviation uses a standard four-level intensity scale for turbulence:

| Intensity | Occupant Effect | Aircraft Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| **Light** | Slight strain on seatbelts; drinks may spill | Minor altitude/attitude changes |
| **Moderate** | Definite strain; unsecured items move | Significant altitude/attitude deviations; control maintained |
| **Severe** | Occupants thrown against restraints; possible injury | Large deviations; control difficult temporarily |
| **Extreme** | Occupants may be injured; structural damage possible | Aircraft momentarily out of control |

Light turbulence is routine; moderate is uncomfortable but manageable; severe requires immediate action; extreme is rare and dangerous.

---

**Turbulence Forecasts and Reporting**

Turbulence is reported and forecast through:

- **PIREPs** — Pilot Reports: Real-time reports from pilots in the air. The most current and accurate turbulence data available. File a PIREP whenever you encounter moderate or greater turbulence.
- **GFA (Icing/Turbulence/Freezing Level chart)** — Forecasts turbulence areas with intensity (LGT, MOD, SEV) and altitude range.
- **SIGMETs** — Issued for severe or extreme turbulence affecting all aircraft.
- **AIRMETs** — Issued for moderate turbulence affecting light aircraft.

---

**Pilot Response to Turbulence**

**In expected turbulence** (convective, frontal):
- Slow to maneuvering speed (VA) or turbulence penetration speed before entering the area
- Ensure seatbelts are fastened
- Stow loose items

**In unexpected turbulence**:
1. Reduce to VA immediately — this is the maximum speed at which full deflection of a single control will not overstress the aircraft
2. Maintain wings-level attitude; accept altitude deviations rather than chasing altitude aggressively
3. Do not make large, rapid control inputs
4. If convective turbulence: look for visual cues (cumulus) and route around if possible
5. File a PIREP for pilots behind you

**Maneuvering speed (VA)**: Specified in the aircraft's Pilot Operating Handbook (POH). Decreases with aircraft weight — a lightly loaded aircraft has a lower VA. Slowing below VA does not make the aircraft immune to turbulence, but it ensures any single control input stays within structural limits.

---

## Key Points

- **Convective turbulence**: thermal activity on hot sunny afternoons; associated with cumulus clouds
- **Mechanical turbulence**: wind over terrain or obstructions; scales with wind speed; worst on the lee side
- **Mountain wave turbulence**: severe; extends well downwind of terrain; rotor zone is most dangerous
- **Clear air turbulence (CAT)**: near jet streams; no visual warning; not detectable by weather radar
- Turbulence intensity: **Light → Moderate → Severe → Extreme**
- In turbulence: reduce to **VA (maneuvering speed)** and maintain wings-level attitude; accept altitude deviations
- File a **PIREP** after encountering moderate or greater turbulence
