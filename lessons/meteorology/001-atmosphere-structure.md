# Lesson MET-001: The Atmosphere

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 001  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.0

---

## Narration Script

Welcome to Meteorology. This is the first of fourteen lessons on weather — a subject that will occupy a significant portion of your Transport Canada written exam and, more importantly, will inform every real-world flight decision you make. Let's start at the foundation: the atmosphere itself.

---

The atmosphere is the envelope of gases that surrounds the Earth. It's held in place by gravity and has no sharp upper boundary — it simply thins out with altitude until it merges with the near-vacuum of space. For aviation purposes, the atmosphere is divided into layers, and understanding those layers helps explain why weather behaves the way it does.

**The Troposphere** is where you live, fly, and where all meaningful weather occurs. It extends from the surface to roughly 36,000 feet (about 11 km) at mid-latitudes, though this boundary — called the tropopause — is higher over the tropics (up to 55,000 feet) and lower over the poles (as low as 25,000 feet). The troposphere contains about 75% of the total mass of the atmosphere and nearly all of the water vapour that drives weather.

The key characteristic of the troposphere is that **temperature decreases with altitude**. On average, the temperature drops about 2°C for every 1,000 feet you climb. This is called the **environmental lapse rate**. The word "lapse" means decrease. The standard lapse rate of 2°C per 1,000 feet is the ISA — International Standard Atmosphere — value. Actual lapse rates in the real atmosphere vary constantly, and that variation determines whether the atmosphere is stable or unstable — a concept we'll return to in the thunderstorms lesson.

**The Tropopause** is the boundary between the troposphere and the layer above it. At the tropopause, the temperature stops decreasing and levels off or even begins to rise. This acts as a natural cap on weather — thunderstorm tops flatten out here because the air above them is no longer cooler than the storm tops. For a PPL candidate, the main takeaway is: the tropopause marks the top of the weather-generating layer.

**The Stratosphere** lies above the tropopause, from roughly 36,000 to 165,000 feet. Temperature is relatively stable or increases slightly with altitude in the lower stratosphere. Very little weather occurs here. Commercial aircraft sometimes cruise in the lower stratosphere; piston-engine general aviation aircraft do not reach it.

Above the stratosphere are the mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere — progressively higher and thinner layers. These have no practical significance for PPL operations.

---

**The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)**

The ISA is a mathematical model of "average" atmospheric conditions used as a reference for instrument calibration, performance charts, and altimetry. You need to know three ISA sea-level values cold:

- **Temperature:** 15°C
- **Pressure:** 1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg)
- **Lapse rate:** 2°C per 1,000 feet (up to the tropopause)

When a performance chart says "standard conditions," it means ISA. When the actual temperature is warmer than ISA, the air is less dense — this degrades engine performance and increases takeoff distances. This is the concept of density altitude, which we'll cover in the General Knowledge section.

---

**Atmospheric Pressure**

Pressure is simply the weight of the air column above a given point. At sea level, that column is very heavy — all the atmosphere above is pressing down. As you climb, there is less air above you, so pressure decreases. At approximately 18,000 feet ASL, atmospheric pressure is roughly half the sea-level value.

This is directly relevant to your altimeter, which is essentially a pressure gauge calibrated to indicate altitude. The altimeter does not measure how high you are — it measures how much air pressure is above you, and converts that to a height using the ISA model. That's why you must update your altimeter setting as pressure changes. We'll cover altimetry in depth in the Air Law section.

---

**Composition of the Atmosphere**

Air is approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and 1% argon and other trace gases. The oxygen fraction stays roughly constant up through the useful range of general aviation operations. What changes with altitude is the pressure — there are fewer molecules per cubic metre of air at altitude, so even though the percentage of oxygen is the same, each breath delivers less oxygen. This leads to hypoxia at higher altitudes. For unpressurized piston aircraft, supplemental oxygen is required above 13,000 feet ASL for prolonged flight under CARs.

---

**Why This Matters for the Exam**

Exam questions on the atmosphere test whether you know the ISA values, the standard lapse rate, which layer weather occurs in, and basic pressure/altitude relationships. Memorize: troposphere, tropopause, 15°C at sea level, 2°C per 1,000 feet, 1013.25 hPa. Everything else in meteorology builds from these fundamentals.

---

## Key Points

- The **troposphere** is the lowest atmospheric layer; all weather and VFR operations occur here
- Temperature decreases with altitude in the troposphere at the standard lapse rate of **2°C per 1,000 feet**
- ISA sea-level values: **15°C**, **1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg)**
- The **tropopause** caps weather systems — thunderstorm tops anvil out at this boundary
- Atmospheric **pressure decreases with altitude** — the altimeter is a pressure instrument
- Air composition is ~78% N₂, ~21% O₂; hypoxia risk increases above 13,000 feet ASL (CARs 605.31)
