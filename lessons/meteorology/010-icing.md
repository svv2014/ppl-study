# Lesson MET-008: Icing

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 008  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.6

---

## Narration Script

Icing is one of the most insidious hazards in Canadian aviation, precisely because it can develop quickly, accumulate silently, and change the aerodynamic character of an aircraft before the pilot fully appreciates the danger. This lesson covers the types of icing, how they form, their effects on aircraft, and what to do when icing is encountered.

---

**What Is Structural Icing**

Structural icing occurs when supercooled water droplets — water that remains in liquid state despite being below 0°C — contact the aircraft and freeze on impact. The result is ice accumulating on wings, tail surfaces, propeller, engine inlets, antennas, and other protrusions.

The key condition: **supercooled liquid water**. This exists in clouds between 0°C and approximately –40°C. Below –40°C, water freezes spontaneously and clouds are composed entirely of ice crystals, which generally do not adhere to aircraft surfaces in significant quantities. So the **icing zone is 0°C to –40°C**, with the most severe icing typically between 0°C and –20°C where liquid water content is highest.

Visible moisture is required for significant structural icing. You can fly through ice-crystal cloud without accumulating significant ice — but flying through freezing rain or supercooled liquid cloud produces rapid accumulation.

---

**Types of Ice**

**Clear Ice (Glaze Ice)**
- Forms from large supercooled droplets (freezing rain, freezing drizzle) at temperatures near 0°C
- Droplets spread back along the airfoil before freezing
- Result: dense, smooth, hard, transparent coating
- Runs back past the protected area of deicing boots — difficult to remove
- Most aerodynamically harmful: alters airfoil shape dramatically, increases weight and drag, reduces lift

**Rime Ice**
- Forms from small supercooled droplets at temperatures –10°C to –20°C
- Droplets freeze instantly on contact
- Result: rough, white, opaque, porous accumulation on leading edges
- Less aerodynamically harmful than clear ice, but still disrupts airflow
- Lighter than clear ice; can often be broken off by deicing boots

**Mixed Ice**
- A combination of clear and rime ice
- Forms in clouds with a range of droplet sizes and temperatures
- Has characteristics of both — heavy accumulation, rough surface
- Unpredictable and difficult to combat

**Frost**
- Forms by sublimation — water vapour deposits directly as ice on surfaces that are below 0°C
- Occurs on the ground overnight, on cold-soaked aircraft arriving from high altitude
- Feathery, crystalline appearance
- **Critical point:** Even a thin layer of frost on wings before takeoff must be removed. It disrupts boundary layer airflow over the wing, significantly reducing the coefficient of lift and increasing stall speed. CARs 602.11 prohibits flight takeoff with frost, ice, or snow on critical surfaces.

---

**Effects on Aircraft**

Ice accumulation affects every aspect of performance:

- **Lift decreases** — Even small amounts of ice on the leading edge disrupt the laminar airflow boundary layer, causing earlier flow separation. A quarter inch of rime ice on the leading edge can reduce lift by up to 30% and increase drag by 40%.
- **Stall speed increases** — The wing stalls at a higher speed due to the degraded airfoil shape.
- **Weight increases** — Ice is heavy. Significant accumulations add meaningful weight on small aircraft.
- **Control authority decreases** — Ice on control surfaces, especially the horizontal tail, can lead to tailplane stall — an insidious condition where the pilot pulls back and the nose drops further.
- **Propeller efficiency decreases** — Ice on propeller blades reduces thrust and can cause vibration if asymmetric. Propeller ice shedding can strike the fuselage violently.
- **Engine induction icing** — Ice can block air inlets, reducing manifold pressure.

**Tailplane stall** is a particularly dangerous form of icing where ice accumulates on the horizontal tail faster than on the wing (the tail has a smaller chord and higher angle of attack relative to the downwash). At a critical point, extending flaps increases downwash on the tail, which combined with the degraded iced surface, causes the tail to stall — manifesting as a sudden pitch-down. Recovery requires reducing flaps and forward pressure. This behaviour is opposite to a normal wing stall recovery.

---

**Carburetor Icing**

Carburetor icing is distinct from structural icing and can occur in apparently benign conditions. In a piston engine with a float-type carburetor, the venturi effect and fuel vaporization cause a temperature drop of up to 25–30°C at the throttle plate. Moisture in the air can freeze here even when OAT is as warm as +35°C in humid conditions.

Signs of carb icing in fixed-pitch aircraft: unexplained drop in RPM, rough running. In constant-speed aircraft: drop in manifold pressure.

**Action:** Apply carb heat immediately. Briefly expect a further RPM drop as the ice melts and passes through the engine, then RPM should recover. Leave carb heat on until the engine runs smoothly. Carb icing is most likely at partial throttle settings — during descent and approach are high-risk phases.

**Prevention:** Apply carb heat proactively during power reductions and when operating in conditions of visible moisture and temperatures +5°C to +25°C.

---

**Icing in Weather Forecasts**

Icing conditions are highlighted in:
- **GFA** (Graphical Forecast for Aviation) — shows icing areas by altitude
- **AIRMET** (Sierra series) — issued for moderate icing affecting light aircraft
- **SIGMET** — issued for severe icing
- **PIREPs** — real-time pilot reports; most valuable for icing intensity

**Icing intensity:**
- **Trace** — Ice detectable, does not require deicing equipment
- **Light** — Ice accumulates; periodic deicing cycling necessary
- **Moderate** — Short encounter hazardous; deicing may not keep up
- **Severe** — Equipment overwhelmed; immediate exit from conditions required

Aircraft without de-/anti-icing equipment should treat moderate or severe icing forecasts as no-go conditions. Under CARs, only aircraft with appropriate certification may operate in **known icing conditions**.

---

## Key Points

- Structural icing requires **supercooled liquid water**: temperature range **0°C to –40°C**
- **Clear (glaze) ice** is most hazardous — from large droplets near 0°C, dense, hard, alters airfoil shape
- **Rime ice** — small droplets, rough white accumulation, less aerodynamically harmful
- **Frost before takeoff must be removed** — reduces lift, raises stall speed (CARs 602.11)
- **Carburetor icing** can occur at OAT up to +35°C in humid conditions — apply carb heat at first sign of power loss
- **Tailplane stall** — iced horizontal stabilizer can pitch the nose down on flap extension
- Only aircraft certified for known icing may legally enter known icing conditions (CARs)
