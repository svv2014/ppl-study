# Lesson MET-012: Wind Shear and Microburst

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 012  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.11

---

## Narration Script

Wind shear and microbursts are among the most operationally dangerous weather phenomena for pilots at any experience level. Both can occur with little visual warning, act in seconds, and have caused fatal accidents worldwide — including to experienced airline crews. Understanding exactly how they work is essential to recognizing the conditions that produce them and responding correctly if you encounter one.

---

**Wind Shear — The Basics**

Wind shear is a change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance. It can be:
- **Horizontal wind shear** — wind changing with horizontal distance
- **Vertical wind shear** — wind changing with altitude (the most critical for flight)

The hazard comes from the *suddenness* and *magnitude* of the change. If the wind changes gradually, the aircraft has time to re-establish a stable flight path. When the change is abrupt — over a few seconds — the aircraft experiences a sudden change in its aerodynamic environment that it may not be able to overcome, particularly at slow speed near the ground.

**How Wind Shear Affects Aircraft**

Consider an aircraft on final approach at 70 knots indicated, wings level, on the glidepath. If the aircraft suddenly enters a region where the wind changes from a 15-knot headwind to calm, the airspeed drops immediately to 55 knots. The wings are now generating less lift. The aircraft sinks. Near the ground, there is no room to recover if the pilot doesn't react instantly.

Conversely, flying into an increasing headwind causes a temporary airspeed increase — the aircraft momentarily gains energy, which can put it above the glidepath. The pilot naturally reduces power. Then, when the aircraft exits the headwind zone, airspeed drops — but now power is reduced and the aircraft is low-energy. This is the classic wind shear trap: the energy gained in the headwind zone lulls the pilot into a low-power state, which then becomes insufficient when the wind shifts.

---

**Low-Level Wind Shear (LLWS)**

Low-level wind shear (LLWS) is defined as wind shear occurring at or below 2,000 feet AGL — the critical band covering approach, landing, takeoff, and initial climbout.

**Causes of LLWS:**

1. **Frontal boundaries** — The most common cause. As a front passes, wind speed and direction change abruptly. If a front passes while you're on approach, the wind conditions during the approach may be entirely different from those when you land.

2. **Temperature inversions** — A strong temperature inversion (where temperature increases with altitude rather than decreasing) can create a shear zone between two layers of air moving at very different speeds. Common at night when surface cooling creates a stable surface layer while winds aloft remain strong.

3. **Thunderstorm outflow** — The gust front ahead of a thunderstorm contains rapidly diverging outflow air. The boundary between calm air ahead and the outflow can produce extreme LLWS. This gust front can precede the visible storm by 10–20 km.

4. **Sea breeze / land breeze** — Coastal areas experience regular wind shear at the boundary between land and sea air masses, particularly in the afternoon.

5. **Terrain channelling** — Valleys and passes channel and accelerate wind, creating shear at the edges of the channel.

**Indicators of LLWS on Approach:**
- Unusual airspeed fluctuations during approach (more than ±10 knots)
- Unusual altitude deviations on a stabilized approach
- PIREPs from preceding aircraft reporting airspeed changes
- Rapidly changing METAR winds or SPECI wind reports
- Wind direction split between surface METAR and ATIS

---

**Microburst**

A microburst is an intense, small-scale downdraft produced by a convective storm (thunderstorm or strong shower). The downdraft descends rapidly, strikes the surface, and diverges outward in all directions in a radial burst — hence the name.

**Size and duration:**
- Typically 2–4 km in diameter (sometimes up to 8 km — a "macroburst")
- Core downdraft can reach 6,000 feet per minute
- Outflow winds at the surface may exceed 60 knots
- Duration: 2–5 minutes (most intense phase), up to 15 minutes total
- Moves with the parent storm at typically 5–20 knots

**The Microburst Hazard on Approach**

The reason microbursts are so dangerous is that an aircraft flying through one on approach encounters three distinct phases in rapid succession:

1. **Increasing headwind** — As the aircraft enters the outflow, it encounters an increasing headwind from the diverging outflow air. Airspeed increases; the aircraft rises above the glidepath. Pilots instinctively reduce power.

2. **Downdraft core** — The aircraft passes through the descending core air. The downward air movement pushes the aircraft toward the ground.

3. **Increasing tailwind** — The aircraft exits the near side of the outflow into a tailwind. Airspeed decreases rapidly. The aircraft loses lift just as it is close to the ground — with reduced power (from phase 1) and a sudden airspeed deficit.

The result: a sudden, uncontrolled descent into the ground. This three-phase sequence has been the cause of multiple fatal approach accidents.

**Virga — The Invisible Microburst Indicator**

Virga is precipitation (rain or snow) falling from a cloud base that evaporates before reaching the ground. You see streaks hanging from the cloud base but no rain at the surface. This is particularly hazardous because:
- The evaporating precipitation cools the air dramatically (evaporative cooling)
- This cooling accelerates the downdraft
- The resulting microburst hits the surface with no rain visible to alert pilots

A virga-producing shower below a storm cell is a high-risk microburst indicator. Treat any approach below a virga shower as potentially subject to an invisible microburst.

---

**Recognizing and Avoiding Microbursts**

**Avoidance on the ground:**
- Check weather products for convective activity (TS, CB, heavy showers) near the airport
- Check PIREPS for reports of airspeed fluctuations on approach
- Request a delay if a convective cell is within 10 nm of the airport and moving toward the runway
- Observe the ATIS/automated surface winds for rapid changes — an anemometer showing sudden gusts or direction changes is a LLWS indicator

**Avoidance in the air:**
- On approach, if airspeed increases suddenly by 15+ knots without a commanded change — consider this a LLWS warning; add power and be prepared to go-around
- On approach, if the vertical speed suddenly increases well beyond the stabilized approach rate — add full power and go-around immediately
- Do not attempt to press through a microburst on short final — the energy deficit at the far edge is catastrophic

**Standard go-around cue:** If airspeed deviates more than ±10 knots from target airspeed, or if sink rate becomes uncontrollable on approach, execute a go-around.

---

**Weather Products for LLWS and Microbursts**

- **AIRMET** — Issued for significant LLWS at or below 2,000 feet AGL
- **SIGMET** — Issued for severe LLWS
- **TAF remarks** — Gust fronts (GF) and LLWS may appear in remarks: `LLWS –5KT/2000FT`
- **METAR** — Wind shift and sudden gust increase indicate a passing gust front or LLWS
- **PIREP** — Most current indicator; file one if you encounter airspeed fluctuations on approach
- **Radar** — Convective cells near the airport visible on weather radar are a LLWS/microburst risk

---

## Key Points

- **Wind shear** = abrupt change in wind speed/direction; most hazardous on approach and takeoff (LLWS ≤ 2,000 feet AGL)
- **Microburst** = intense localized downdraft that diverges at the surface; typical diameter 2–4 km; lasts < 15 minutes
- Approach through a microburst: **headwind gain → downdraft → sudden tailwind** = catastrophic airspeed and lift loss
- **Virga** (precipitation evaporating before reaching the ground) is a high-risk microburst indicator
- **LLWS indicators**: unexpected airspeed swings >10 knots, unusual glidepath deviations, PIREPs, rapidly changing surface winds
- If airspeed fluctuates ±10 knots unexpectedly on approach → **go-around**; do not press through
- Convective cells and gust fronts near the airport = **delay or divert**; do not attempt approach
