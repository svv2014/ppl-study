# Lesson MET-004: Moisture and Clouds

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 004  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.2

---

## Narration Script

Water in the atmosphere — as vapour, liquid, and ice — is the source of nearly all aviation weather hazards. Clouds reduce visibility, carry ice, hide terrain, and spawn thunderstorms. Understanding how moisture behaves and how clouds form is the bridge between the physics of the atmosphere and the practical hazards you'll face as a pilot.

---

**Water Vapour and Humidity**

Air can hold water vapour, but its capacity to do so is temperature-dependent: warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air. **Relative humidity** expresses how much moisture the air holds as a percentage of its maximum capacity at that temperature. At 100% relative humidity, the air is said to be **saturated**.

The **dewpoint temperature** (Td) is the temperature to which air must be cooled — at constant pressure — before it becomes saturated. When the air temperature (T) equals the dewpoint (Td), condensation occurs: water vapour condenses into tiny liquid droplets forming cloud, fog, or dew.

The **temperature-dewpoint spread** (T – Td) tells you how close the air is to saturation. A spread of 2°C or less is the threshold where pilots expect fog or low cloud. As the spread approaches zero, visibility deteriorates rapidly.

---

**How Clouds Form**

Clouds form when moist air is cooled to its dewpoint. The main cooling mechanisms are:

1. **Lifting** — Air forced upward over terrain (orographic lifting), along a frontal surface, or by convective thermals cools as it expands. This is the most common cloud-forming process.
2. **Radiation cooling** — The surface cools at night by emitting heat. Air in contact with the cooled surface also cools; if it cools below its dewpoint, radiation fog forms (covered in MET-009).
3. **Advection** — Warm, moist air moves over a cold surface and is cooled from below.

As air rises, it cools at the **dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR)** of approximately 3°C per 1,000 feet until saturation is reached. Once saturated, condensation releases latent heat, slowing the cooling to the **saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR)** of roughly 1.5°C to 2°C per 1,000 feet. This is why rising moist air in an unstable atmosphere can continue rising vigorously — latent heat release keeps the parcel warmer than the surrounding air.

**Estimating convective cloud base:** For cumuliform clouds, a useful approximation is:

> Cloud base (feet AGL) ≈ (T – Td) × 400

If temperature is 22°C and dewpoint is 12°C, the spread is 10°C, giving a cloud base of about 4,000 feet AGL. This formula is tested on the PPL exam.

---

**Cloud Classification**

Clouds are classified by altitude (height of the base) and form (shape).

**By altitude:**
- **High clouds** (above ~20,000 feet) — Cirrus, Cirrocumulus, Cirrostratus — composed of ice crystals
- **Middle clouds** (6,500–20,000 feet) — Altocumulus, Altostratus — liquid water or mixed phase
- **Low clouds** (surface to ~6,500 feet) — Stratus, Stratocumulus, Nimbostratus — liquid water
- **Vertically developed** (any altitude to tropopause) — Cumulus, Cumulonimbus

**By form:**
- **Cumulus (heap)** — Formed by convection in unstable air; distinct vertical development; showery precipitation
- **Stratus (layer)** — Formed in stable air; horizontal layers; steady, widespread precipitation or drizzle
- **Cirrus** — High, thin ice-crystal clouds; indicates approaching fronts when thickening
- **Nimbostratus** — Dark, thick stratus producing continuous precipitation; low ceilings and poor visibility
- **Cumulonimbus (Cb)** — The most severe cloud; extends from low levels to the tropopause; contains thunderstorms, severe icing, hail, and extreme turbulence

---

**Cloud Descriptors in Weather Reports**

Clouds are reported in METARs using these descriptors (from lowest to highest coverage):
- **FEW** — 1–2 eighths (oktas) of sky coverage
- **SCT** (Scattered) — 3–4 eighths
- **BKN** (Broken) — 5–7 eighths — constitutes a **ceiling**
- **OVC** (Overcast) — 8 eighths — constitutes a **ceiling**

A ceiling is the lowest layer reported as BKN or OVC. VFR cloud clearance requirements apply to ceiling height. Under CARs 602.114–602.115, in controlled airspace the VFR ceiling minimum is generally 1,500 feet AGL.

**Special designators:**
- **CB** — Cumulonimbus embedded or towering
- **TCU** — Towering Cumulus; precursor to Cb development
- **VV** — Vertical Visibility; used when the sky is obscured (as in fog or heavy precipitation)

---

**Aviation Significance**

- **Icing:** Any cloud at temperatures between 0°C and –40°C may contain supercooled liquid water — water that remains liquid despite being below freezing. When supercooled droplets contact the airframe, they freeze on contact. This is structural icing. The most severe icing environments are cumulonimbus, nimbostratus with embedded CB, and freezing rain situations.

- **Turbulence:** Cumulus and cumulonimbus indicate convective activity and turbulence. Even beneath a Cu, thermals can give a rough ride at low altitude.

- **Visibility:** Stratus, fog, and nimbostratus reduce visibility dramatically. A low stratus layer can bring ceilings below VFR minimums with no precipitation.

---

## Key Points

- **Dewpoint** is the temperature at which air reaches 100% relative humidity and condensation begins
- T–Td spread ≤ 2°C → fog or low cloud likely
- Convective cloud base (feet AGL) ≈ **(T – Td) × 400**
- **Cumulus** = unstable air, showery precip; **Stratus** = stable air, steady precip or drizzle
- **Cumulonimbus** is the most hazardous cloud: severe icing, extreme turbulence, hail
- In METARs: **BKN** or **OVC** = ceiling; **FEW/SCT** = not a ceiling
- Supercooled liquid water in clouds below 0°C causes structural icing
