# Lesson MET-009: Fog

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 009  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.7

---

## Narration Script

Fog is among the most common weather hazards for general aviation in Canada. It can reduce visibility from miles to near zero in minutes, and unlike cloud — which you can often fly above — fog sits right at the surface, where you are taking off and landing. Knowing how fog forms, what type you're dealing with, and when it will dissipate is critical to safe operations.

---

**What Is Fog**

Fog is simply a cloud whose base is at the surface, reducing visibility to less than 1 statute mile (1.6 km). When the reduced visibility is 1 to 6 statute miles, it is called **mist** (or haze if dry particles are the cause). The distinction matters in weather reports: METAR codes `FG` for fog (visibility < 1 SM), and `BR` for mist (1–6 SM visibility).

The physical process is the same as cloud formation: air is cooled to its dewpoint (or moisture is added until saturation). The difference is location — fog is at the surface.

---

**Types of Fog**

**Radiation Fog (Ground Fog)**

The most common type in Canada, especially inland. Conditions:
- Clear sky (allows maximum radiative heat loss from the surface overnight)
- Light to calm winds (< 5 knots; stronger winds mix the surface layer and prevent cooling)
- High relative humidity at sunset (small T–Td spread)
- Long nights (autumn and early spring are prime fog seasons)

The surface cools by radiating heat into space. The air touching the cool surface cools below its dewpoint. Fog forms in low-lying areas first — valleys, river bottoms — and can spread to cover large areas by dawn.

**Dissipation:** After sunrise, solar heating warms the ground, which warms the fog from below. Fog burns off from the ground up — you may see a thin overcast aloft that is the top of the fog layer burning off from below. Expect dissipation 1–3 hours after sunrise. Key exam point: fog dissipates from the **bottom up** due to solar heating.

**Advection Fog**

Forms when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a colder surface. Common scenarios in Canada:
- Warm Pacific air moving over cold British Columbia coastal waters or snow-covered land
- Warm Gulf air moving into the Maritimes over cold ocean water in spring/summer
- Maritime air moving over cold inland surfaces in late winter

Unlike radiation fog, advection fog can form and persist with winds of 10–20 knots, doesn't require a clear sky, and can persist through the day without solar heating to dissipate it. It can cover a very large geographic area.

**Steam Fog (Arctic Sea Smoke)**

Forms when very cold air (usually Arctic) flows over relatively warm open water. The water is warm enough to evaporate vigorously; the cold air immediately above becomes saturated and fog forms in wispy columns (resembling steam). Common in autumn over lakes and rivers in Canada before freeze-up, and in coastal areas with cold air outbreaks. Generally shallow and limited to open water areas.

**Upslope Fog (Orographic Fog)**

Moist air forced uphill cools adiabatically. When the temperature reaches the dewpoint, fog (or low cloud) forms. Common on the windward slopes of terrain. On the Canadian Prairies, moist air flowing from the east up the gentle slope toward the Rockies can produce extensive upslope fog. Persists as long as the onshore wind continues.

**Precipitation-Induced Fog (Frontal Fog)**

Precipitation falling through dry air evaporates, increasing the moisture content of the air below the cloud base. Eventually the sub-cloud air becomes saturated, and fog forms. This is common ahead of warm fronts where steady rain falls through a cold, nearly-saturated air mass near the surface. The combination of low cloud and fog makes these situations very challenging for VFR operations.

**Ice Fog**

At temperatures below –30°C to –40°C, water vapour can deposit directly as ice crystals in the air near the surface. Ice fog is common in the Canadian North and the Prairie provinces during extreme cold outbreaks. It reduces visibility significantly and deposits rime on aircraft surfaces. Engine exhaust can contribute to ice fog in calm conditions around airports.

---

**Fog Forecasting and Indicators**

Key indicators that fog may develop overnight:

1. Small T–Td spread at sunset (within 2–3°C)
2. Clear sky forecast overnight
3. Light winds forecast
4. Recent precipitation wetting the surface (high surface moisture)

If the spread is 2°C or less at sunset with clear skies and calm winds, radiation fog is virtually certain.

In METARs, fog is coded:
- `FG` — Fog (visibility < 1 SM)
- `MIFG` — Shallow fog (surface-based, tops < 6 feet — aircraft can often see above it)
- `BCFG` — Patchy fog
- `FZFG` — Freezing fog (fog at or below 0°C, deposits rime ice on aircraft)

---

**Operational Significance**

VFR pilots cannot fly in fog — it immediately reduces visibility below VFR minimums. Even when departing from an airport with marginal visibility, you must ensure your destination will have VFR conditions on arrival and that alternates are available.

**Freezing fog** (FZFG) combines the vision hazard with a structural icing hazard. It deposits rime ice on all exposed surfaces including wings, windshield, and pitot-static probes. A pitot tube frozen by freezing fog will cause airspeed indicator failure.

**Planning tips:**
- Check T–Td spread at sunset for departure airport and destination
- Review TAF for fog remarks (TEMPO FZFG, BECMG MIFG, etc.)
- Know the typical fog dissipation time for your destination if morning fog is forecast
- Have a fog-free alternate planned when fog is possible at destination

---

## Key Points

- Fog = cloud with base at the surface, visibility < 1 SM; Mist (`BR`) = 1–6 SM
- **Radiation fog**: clear sky, calm winds, high humidity, long night — dissipates from **bottom up** after sunrise
- **Advection fog**: warm moist air over cold surface — can persist in moderate winds through daytime
- **Steam fog**: cold air over warm water — shallow, over water only
- **Upslope fog**: moist air forced up terrain — persists as long as upslope wind continues
- T–Td spread ≤ 2°C at sunset with clear skies and calm winds = fog very likely
- **Freezing fog** (FZFG) ices the aircraft — particularly hazardous on the ground and during flight
