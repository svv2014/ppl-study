# Lesson MET-006: Air Masses and Fronts

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 006  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 1.4

---

## Narration Script

Weather doesn't just happen randomly — it's organized into large air masses and the boundaries between them called fronts. If you understand air masses and fronts, you can predict the broad character of the weather over hundreds of kilometres. This is the foundation of flight planning for cross-country trips.

---

**Air Masses**

An air mass is a large body of air — covering hundreds or thousands of kilometres — that has relatively uniform temperature and moisture characteristics throughout. Air masses acquire these properties by sitting over a **source region** for days or weeks. Canada and the northern hemisphere have several key source regions.

**Classification:**

Air masses are classified by two characteristics:
1. **Source region temperature:** Arctic (A), Polar (P), or Tropical (T)
2. **Surface type of source region:** Continental (c) — dry land; Maritime (m) — ocean

Combined, you get types like:
- **cA** — Continental Arctic: extremely cold, dry; forms over the Canadian Arctic in winter; associated with bitter cold outbreaks
- **cP** — Continental Polar: cold, dry; forms over northern continental Canada; the dominant winter air mass
- **mP** — Maritime Polar: cool, moist; forms over cold north Pacific or north Atlantic; brings cloud and rain to coastal areas
- **mT** — Maritime Tropical: warm, moist; forms over the Gulf of Mexico and tropical Atlantic; brings humid, unstable conditions and thunderstorm potential to eastern Canada in summer
- **cT** — Continental Tropical: hot, dry; forms over desert regions; occasionally affects southern Canada

When an air mass moves over a surface warmer than itself, it is heated from below, becomes unstable, and produces cumulus and showery weather — a **cold air mass** behaviour. When an air mass moves over a surface cooler than itself, it is cooled from below, stabilizes, and produces fog or stratus — a **warm air mass** behaviour.

---

**Fronts**

A front is the boundary between two different air masses. The name comes from the military analogy — it's a battlefront between competing air masses. Fronts are where the most significant weather changes occur.

**Cold Front**

A cold front is the leading edge of an advancing cold air mass pushing under a warmer air mass. Cold fronts in Canada typically move from northwest to southeast. Key characteristics:

- **Steep slope** (1:50 to 1:100) — cold air wedges under warm air, forcing rapid lifting
- **Fast movement** — cold fronts typically move at 20–35 knots
- **Violent, narrow weather band** — cumulonimbus, heavy showers, thunderstorms, squally winds
- **Rapid change** — the bad weather passes quickly (1–2 hours), followed by rapid clearing, lower temperatures, and rising pressure
- **Wind shift** on passage — winds typically back ahead of the front (southerly) then shift abruptly to northwest or west after passage
- **Pressure** falls ahead, rises behind

Frontal cloud extends 50–200 km ahead of the surface front position.

**Warm Front**

A warm front is the leading edge of an advancing warm air mass riding up over a retreating cold air mass. Key characteristics:

- **Gentle slope** (1:100 to 1:200) — warm air rises gradually
- **Slow movement** — warm fronts move at 10–20 knots
- **Wide weather band** — cloud and precipitation spread 300–1,000 km ahead of the surface front
- **Classic cloud sequence** approaching a warm front: Cirrus → Cirrostratus → Altostratus → Nimbostratus — a textbook pattern for the exam
- **Steady, widespread precipitation** — rain or snow over a large area for many hours
- **Warm sector** follows passage: temperature and dewpoint rise, winds back to southerly, pressure levelling
- **Significant icing hazard** — freezing rain common ahead of warm front in winter, where rain from the warm layer falls into the sub-freezing cold air below

**Stationary Front**

A front that is not moving appreciably. Persistent poor weather — low cloud, drizzle, fog — can persist for days along and ahead of a stationary front. Plan around it; it won't clear quickly.

**Occluded Front**

In a mature low-pressure system, the cold front moves faster than the warm front and eventually catches it. The cold front undercuts the warm air, lifting the entire warm sector off the surface — this is an **occlusion**. Two subtypes exist:
- **Cold-type occlusion** — The air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front (common over Canada)
- **Warm-type occlusion** — The air behind is warmer than the air ahead

Occluded fronts produce complex, persistent weather combining characteristics of both warm and cold fronts.

---

**Frontal Analysis on Weather Charts**

On surface analysis charts:
- Cold front = blue line with filled triangles pointing in the direction of movement
- Warm front = red line with filled semicircles
- Stationary front = alternating blue triangles and red semicircles on opposite sides
- Occluded front = purple line with alternating triangles and semicircles

---

**Practical Flight Planning Implications**

- **Cold front approaching** — Expect deteriorating conditions ahead; plan to be on the ground before the front arrives or well clear. After passage, conditions improve rapidly.
- **Warm front on route** — Expect IFR conditions over a wide area, possible freezing rain at low altitudes. Check whether you can safely fly above the precipitation level.
- **Stationary front** — Treat as a no-go until it clears; conditions can persist indefinitely.
- **Occluded front** — Complex, often with multiple precipitation bands; check GFAs for detail.

---

## Key Points

- Air mass types: **cA** (bitter cold/dry), **cP** (cold/dry), **mP** (cool/moist), **mT** (warm/moist)
- Cold fronts = steep slope, fast-moving, narrow band of severe weather, rapid clearing after
- Warm fronts = gentle slope, slow-moving, wide area of cloud and steady precipitation
- Warm front cloud sequence (approaching): **Cirrus → Cirrostratus → Altostratus → Nimbostratus**
- Occluded front = cold front overtakes warm front, lifts warm sector off surface
- Wind shift and pressure change are key indicators of frontal passage
- Freezing rain hazard is high ahead of warm fronts in winter (cold air trapped at surface)
