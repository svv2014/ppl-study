# Lesson MET-014: Aviation Weather Decision Making

**Section:** Meteorology  
**Lesson number:** 014  
**Estimated time:** 20 minutes  
**Source:** TP 12880E Chapter 8, AIM MET 3.0, CARs 602.71

---

## Narration Script

You've spent thirteen lessons learning the science and products of aviation meteorology. This final lesson ties it all together: how do you use that knowledge to make a safe go/no-go decision before a flight, and what do you do in the air when the weather isn't what you expected? Weather decision making is where knowledge becomes judgment.

---

**The Legal Requirement**

CARs 602.71 is clear: before commencing VFR flight, the pilot-in-command **must** obtain available weather information for the route, including the destination. This is a legal requirement. "Available" means you must make a genuine effort to obtain weather information — you cannot simply claim the phone was unavailable or the internet was down if standard weather resources were accessible.

The minimum weather briefing for a VFR cross-country includes:
1. **METARs** for departure, significant en route points, and destination
2. **TAF** for departure and destination
3. **GFA** (CLOUDS AND WEATHER and ICING/TURBULENCE/FZLVL) for the route
4. **SIGMETs and AIRMETs** for the route
5. **PIREPs** for the area if available
6. **NOTAMs** (including weather-related NOTAMs)

Briefings are available from **NAV CANADA** via the aviation weather website, the 1800wxbrief service, or directly through a FIC (Flight Information Centre).

---

**The Pre-Flight Go/No-Go Decision**

Weather decision making is not binary — it's a risk assessment. The framework:

**Step 1 — Get the picture.** Review all weather products systematically. Don't cherry-pick the good news.

**Step 2 — Identify the limiting factors.** What's the ceiling at the destination? Is icing forecast at your cruise altitude? Are there thunderstorms in the area? Will the freezing level be below your route?

**Step 3 — Assess margins.** The legal VFR minimum is not a target — it's a cliff edge. Aim for significant margin above legal minimums. If the TAF shows 1,500 feet and 5 SM at your destination, that's technically legal VFR controlled airspace — but your margin above the 1,000-foot legal minimum is only 500 feet. If the forecast is off, you're immediately in IMC.

**Step 4 — Apply personal minimums.** Personal minimums are conservative, self-imposed weather limits based on your experience level, currency, and the specific flight. Examples for a newly licensed PPL:
- Minimum ceiling: 3,000 feet
- Minimum visibility: 5 SM
- No flight with embedded CB or TS anywhere within 20 nm of the route
- No flight in forecast icing

Personal minimums should be set on the ground, not re-negotiated in the air.

**Step 5 — Plan alternates and gates.** Identify an alternate airport you can reach if the destination deteriorates. Set a "gate" — a point in time or position at which you will reassess and divert if conditions aren't as forecast. "If it's not VFR at the destination when I'm 50 nm out, I turn for the alternate."

---

**Automatic No-Go Conditions**

Some conditions should be immediate no-go decisions for basic VFR pilots in non-IFR-equipped, non-FIKI aircraft:

- **Freezing rain (FZRA) or freezing drizzle (FZDZ)** anywhere along the route at planned altitude — structural icing is catastrophic
- **Active thunderstorms (TS, Cb)** within 20 nm of the route — severe turbulence, hail, lightning, microbursts
- **Forecast conditions below VFR minimums** at destination with no accessible alternate
- **Forecast IMC en route** (cloud layers below safe terrain clearance altitude)
- **SIGMETs for severe icing or severe turbulence** on the route

---

**In-Flight Weather Decision Making**

The pre-flight no-go decision is the easiest. Harder is the in-flight decision when the weather doesn't match the forecast.

**The most dangerous pilot behaviour: continuing VFR into IMC.**

VFR-into-IMC (flying into clouds or below minimums) is the leading cause of fatal general aviation accidents in Canada. The typical scenario: VFR pilot encounters deteriorating visibility or lowering ceilings, continues hoping conditions will improve, eventually enters cloud, loses spatial orientation, and loses control.

**The Three W's of VFR-into-IMC:**
1. **Where is the IMC?** — Can you see it ahead? How close?
2. **What are my options?** — Turn back, divert, descend, climb?
3. **When must I decide?** — Don't wait until you're in the cloud to act

**The 180° Turn:** At the first indication that conditions are deteriorating below your personal minimums, turn around. This is never the wrong call. A 180° turn in VFR is an easy manoeuvre; executing an immediate turn in IMC without instrument training is often fatal.

---

**Hazardous Attitudes in Weather Flying**

Transport Canada and ICAO identify five hazardous attitudes that lead to poor weather decisions:

1. **Anti-authority** — "The rules don't apply to me." Ignoring weather minimums.
2. **Impulsivity** — "I need to do something now." Making hasty decisions without gathering all weather information.
3. **Invulnerability** — "It won't happen to me." Dismissing risks because previous flights in marginal conditions went fine.
4. **Macho** — "I can handle it." Pressing into conditions beyond actual capability.
5. **Resignation** — "There's nothing I can do." Passively accepting a deteriorating situation instead of acting decisively.

**Get-there-itis** (press-on-itis) is perhaps the most common weather-related hazardous attitude: continuing a flight into deteriorating conditions because of schedule pressure, passenger expectations, or unwillingness to admit the flight can't be completed. It has killed more pilots than almost any other single factor.

The antidote: **decide on the ground** what your limits are. When you're in the air and conditions are deteriorating, you have already pre-decided to turn around before reaching those limits. Remove the in-flight renegotiation.

---

**Practical Summary: The Weather Briefing Checklist**

Before every cross-country VFR flight:

```
□ METARs for departure, en route, and destination (within 1 hour of planned departure)
□ TAF for destination (valid through planned arrival + 1 hour)
□ GFA – CLD/WX chart for route (valid time closest to departure)
□ GFA – ICG/TURB/FZLVL chart for route (check freezing level vs. planned altitude)
□ SIGMETs – any active for the route?
□ AIRMETs – any active for the route?
□ PIREPs – recent reports in the area?
□ Thunderstorm potential? CB or TS on any product?
□ Icing – FZRA, FZDZ, or forecast MOD+ icing at planned altitude?
□ Personal minimums – do forecast conditions meet my personal standards?
□ Alternate identified – reachable VFR if destination deteriorates?
□ Go/no-go gate defined for in-flight reassessment
```

If any one box reveals a condition that meets your automatic no-go criteria, the flight does not depart.

---

## Key Points

- CARs 602.71 requires obtaining available weather before VFR flight — it's a legal requirement
- **Personal minimums** should be more conservative than legal minimums; set on the ground
- Automatic no-go conditions: FZRA, active TS within 20 nm of route, conditions below VFR minimums at destination with no alternate
- **VFR-into-IMC** is the leading cause of fatal GA accidents in Canada — the 180° turn is always acceptable
- **Get-there-itis** is the most dangerous hazardous attitude for weather flying
- Complete weather briefing: METARs, TAFs, GFA (both charts), SIGMETs, AIRMETs, PIREPs, NOTAMs
- Set a go/no-go **gate** before departure — a decision point defined in advance
