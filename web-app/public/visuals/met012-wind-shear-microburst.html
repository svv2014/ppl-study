MET-012 — Wind Shear and Microbursts

Transport Canada · TP 12880E Chapter 8 · PPL Written Exam

Wind Shear — Definition and Types
Type Definition Common Sources Aviation Risk
Horizontal Wind Shear Change in wind speed or direction across a horizontal distance at the same altitude Frontal boundaries, jet stream edges, gust fronts En route hazard Heading and airspeed deviations; drift from track
Vertical Wind Shear Change in wind speed or direction with a change in altitude (most critical for approach/departure) Low-level jet, temperature inversion, thunderstorm outflow, microburst Critical on approach Sudden airspeed loss/gain; altitude excursions; potential controlled flight into terrain
Low-Level Wind Shear (LLWS) Wind shear below 2,000 ft AGL — most dangerous phase of flight Microburst, gust front, mountain wave rotor, strong temperature inversion Extreme — takeoff/landing phase Airspeed changes of 20–50 kt in seconds; loss of control close to ground
Microburst — Characteristics
Parameter Value Notes
Diameter Less than 4 km (2 NM) Compact; can be entirely within an airport terminal area; may not trigger all sensors
Duration Less than 15 minutes peak intensity Typically 5–10 minutes of severe conditions; can develop within 5 minutes
Downdraft speed Up to 6,000 fpm (100 ft/sec) Exceeds maximum climb rate of most light aircraft; impossible to out-climb
Outflow speed Up to 45 knots Outflow spreads radially outward from impact point at surface
Wind shear magnitude Up to 90 kt across the microburst Headwind gain entering, then massive tailwind loss exiting — net effect: severe airspeed loss
Most dangerous phase Approach and initial takeoff climb Low altitude with no margin for recovery; initial headwind increase masks hazard
Microburst Encounter on Approach — Phase by Phase
Aircraft on Final Approach Flying Into a Microburst
1
Headwind Increase
Aircraft enters the outflow. Headwind increases suddenly. Airspeed rises (e.g. +15 kt). Aircraft pitches up, climbs above glideslope. Pilot may reduce power to maintain profile. Deceiving — seems OK
2
Downdraft Core
Aircraft enters the downdraft column. Powerful sink up to 6,000 fpm pushes aircraft toward ground. Full power may not arrest descent. Altitude loss is rapid and uncontrollable. Critical phase
3
Tailwind — Airspeed Loss
Aircraft exits the far side. Wind shifts to tailwind. Airspeed drops dramatically (e.g. -30 kt). Aircraft below glideslope, low airspeed, near stall, close to ground. Power at full but insufficient. Maximum danger — CFIT risk
4
Go-Around / Divert
If go-around is initiated: do NOT fly through the downdraft core. Full power, pitch for best angle, turn away from the microburst. If already in core: maximum power, accept sink rate, maintain control. Divert if possible before encounter
Recognition Cues — Microburst Warning Signs
Cue Description Reliability
Rain Shaft / Curtain Visible column of heavy precipitation descending from Cb base to surface; compact and dark Moderate Dry microbursts may lack visible rain shaft
Virga Precipitation streaks falling from cloud base that evaporate before reaching surface High Dry microburst indicator — evaporative cooling drives powerful downdraft; no rain at surface so pilots are surprised
Sudden Airspeed Change Unexpected airspeed increase of > 15 kt on approach not consistent with power setting High Immediate action cue: initiate go-around and report LLWS to ATC
PIREP / ATC Warning Previous aircraft reporting LLWS; ATC LLWS alert system activations Best indicator Take all LLWS PIREPs seriously; conditions change rapidly
Dust Ring Expanding ring of dust or debris at surface below the outflow point Visual only Visible on clear days; not detectable at night or in rain

Wind Shear and Microburst — Avoidance Rules

DO NOTDo not attempt a go-around through the microburst core. The downdraft exceeds aircraft climb performance. Turn laterally to exit the microburst.
DIVERTIf PIREP or ATC reports LLWS on approach, divert to alternate or hold until conditions clear. Microbursts dissipate within 15 minutes typically.
VIRGATreat virga as a dry microburst warning. The evaporative downdraft can be as powerful as a wet microburst but with no visible rain at the surface to warn you.
REPORTIf you encounter LLWS on approach, report it immediately to ATC. This protects following aircraft. Use phraseology: "LLWS, airspeed loss of [X] kt on final."

Memory Hook — Wind Shear Essentials

MICROBURSTDiameter < 4 km, duration < 15 min, downdraft up to 6,000 fpm, outflow up to 45 kt. Small, brief, deadly.
APPROACH SEQUENCEHeadwind gain (misleading) → downdraft core → tailwind/airspeed loss (deadly). The headwind gain masks the danger until it is too late.
VIRGA = DANGERVirga means precipitation evaporating before the surface. The evaporative cooling drives a powerful invisible downdraft — dry microburst. Most insidious type.
GO-AROUNDInitiate go-around at the first sign of unexpected airspeed change on final. Turn laterally — do NOT fly through the downdraft core.
